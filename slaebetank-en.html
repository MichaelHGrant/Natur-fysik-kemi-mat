The wave travels with the ship

A ship pushes water aside at the bow and lets it close in again at the stern. That sets the surface moving, and the waves carry energy away. The engine has to pay for that energy: this is the wave resistance.

The waves just behind the ship move at the ship's speed U. A deep-water wave of wavelength λ has the speed c = √(gλ/2π), so the ship's own waves have the wavelength

λ = 2πU²/g

It grows with the square of the speed. At low speed many short waves fit along the hull. As the speed rises the wave grows longer, and when λ is as long as the ship, the crest of the bow wave is at the bow and the next crest at the stern. That happens at the Froude number

Fn = U/√(gL) = 1/√(2π) ≈ 0.40

This is hull speed, which sailors know as 1.34·√(L in feet) knots. To go faster, the wave must become longer than the hull. The stern sinks into the trough, the bow rises, and the ship climbs its own bow wave. The resistance grows steeply, and in practice a displacement ship cannot go much faster.

Humps and hollows in the resistance

The bow and the stern each make their own wave system. When the crests fall on top of each other, the waves behind the ship are high and the resistance large. When a crest falls on a trough, they partly cancel. That is why the curve of CW has humps and hollows, with the last and largest hump around Fn ≈ 0.5. Naval architects choose the length of the hull so that the ship lies in a hollow at its normal speed.

The bulbous bow

The round bulb below the waterline at the bow of large ships makes its own wave system, shifted by half a wavelength relative to the bow wave. At the right speed the two waves partly cancel. Try the container ship with and without the bulb and watch CW fall at Fn ≈ 0.2. Make the bulb too large, and it makes a big wave of its own instead.

The Kelvin wedge

The waves behind a ship lie inside a wedge with a half-angle of 19.47°, whatever the speed and the size of the ship. Lord Kelvin showed this in 1887 from the dispersion of deep-water waves, and it holds from ducks to aircraft carriers. Inside the wedge you see the transverse waves at right angles to the course, and along the edge the oblique diverging waves.

Froude's towing tank

In 1871 William Froude built a towing tank at Torquay in England. He towed ship models through the water and showed that the waves of the model and of the ship are alike when they have the same Froude number. Friction, on the other hand, scales with the Reynolds number, so the two parts must be calculated separately. Naval architects still work this way.

What the page computes

The hull is described by its half-breadth f(x,z) = ½B·(1 − |2x/L|p)·(1 − |z/T|q) plus a bulb. The wave resistance is found with Michell's integral from 1898, thin-ship theory:

RW = (4ρg²/πU²) ∫1∞ |H(λ)|² λ²/√(λ²−1) dλ

where H(λ) = ∬ ∂f/∂x · eλ²k₀z e−iλk₀x dx dz and k₀ = g/U². The same H gives the wave pattern behind the ship by adding up waves travelling in all directions.

Friction is calculated with the ITTC-1957 line CF = 0.075/(log₁₀Re − 2)² and a form factor 1 + k = 1.1 on the wetted area.

Limitations

Thin-ship theory is linear and assumes a slender hull. It overestimates the wave resistance of beamy, full hulls, gives no sinkage or trim, and does not apply to planing boats that rise up over the wave at high speed.

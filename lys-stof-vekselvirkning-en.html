The interaction of light with matter

What really happens when a light wave hits an atom? Six interactive stations on absorption, emission, scattering, refraction and fluorescence – with animations you control yourself.

01 · Four basic processes

Everything light does when it meets matter can be traced back to four relationships between photon and electron.

When a light wave hits a material, it is ultimately the electric fields in the light that tug at the material's electrons. What happens next depends on the photon's energy compared with the energy levels the electrons can occupy:

Absorption – the photon disappears, and its energy lifts an electron to a higher level.
Emission – an electron falls to a lower level and sends the surplus energy off as a new photon.
Scattering – the photon briefly shakes the electron cloud and is released again in another direction, without being taken up permanently.
Refraction – the oscillating electrons themselves send out tiny light waves, which add to the original wave and slow its progress through the material.

The next stations explore each of these – plus two close relatives: the photoelectric effect and fluorescence.

02 · Absorption & emission – quantum jumps in the hydrogen atom

Click on an energy level to move the electron there, and see which photon is needed.

In Bohr's model of the hydrogen atom the electron can only occupy certain energy levels, given by En = −13.6 eV / n². If the electron moves upwards, it must absorb a photon with exactly the right energy difference. If it moves downwards, it emits a photon with that energy.

Transitions down to n = 1 are called the Lyman series (ultraviolet), down to n = 2 the Balmer series (visible light), and down to n = 3 the Paschen series (infrared).

03 · The photoelectric effect

This is where Einstein convinced the world that light comes in packets.

If you shine light on a metal surface, electrons can be torn loose – but only if the energy of the individual photon exceeds the metal's work function φ. More photons (higher intensity) give more electrons, but never faster electrons. Only the wavelength – the photon's energy – determines the electrons' maximum speed.

04 · Rayleigh scattering – the colour of the sky

Blue light is scattered far more strongly than red – that is why the sky is blue, and the Sun turns red at the horizon.

When light meets molecules that are much smaller than the wavelength, it is scattered with a strength that grows as 1/λ⁴. Blue light (~450 nm) is therefore scattered about four times more strongly than red light (~650 nm).

05 · Why do materials have a refractive index?

A bound electron is a small oscillator – the light drives it, and its response shapes the refractive index.

Think of each electron in the glass as a ball on a spring with its own resonance frequency ω₀ (typically far up in the ultraviolet). The electric field of the light drives the oscillator at its own frequency ω. The oscillator's amplitude and phase shift relative to the driving field determine how much the light is slowed (refractive index n) and how much is absorbed (κ).

The driven, damped electron oscillator

Notice: for ω < ω₀ (to the left of the resonance) n grows with ω – that is why blue light (higher ω) is refracted more than red in the prism you can explore on the optics page. Below resonance the electron follows the field; at resonance it lags 90° behind, and here the absorption κ peaks; above resonance the shift approaches 180°.

06 · Fluorescence – light in, different light out

Why does a highlighter glow bright yellow under UV light, even though it is hit by invisible ultraviolet?

A fluorescent molecule absorbs a high-energy photon (often UV) and jumps up into a high vibrational state of an excited electronic state. The molecule quickly loses a little energy as heat and falls to the bottom of the excited state before it emits a photon back to the ground state. The emitted photon therefore normally has lower energy – and a longer wavelength – than the one that was absorbed. The difference is called the Stokes shift.

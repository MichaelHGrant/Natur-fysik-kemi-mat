Why geese fly in a V

Every wing leaves two vortices behind it, one from each wingtip. Inside them the air flows downwards; outside them it flows upwards. A bird that puts its inner wing in the upwash from the bird ahead gets free lift and saves energy. Here the upwash is computed for every bird while the flock flies. Upwash behind the wingtips

A bird stays up by giving the air a downward momentum. Behind the wing the air rolls up into two vortices, one from each wingtip, turning in opposite directions. Between them, right behind the bird, the air flows downwards. Outside them it flows upwards.

Pushing air downwards costs energy: the induced power. For a wing with elliptic lift distribution, span b and speed U, the downwash at the wing is

w₀ = Γ / (2b),   Γ = mg / (ρU·πb/4),   Pi = mg·w₀

If another bird flies in an upwash w, it only has to give the air w₀ − w, and its induced power falls by the fraction w/w₀. The simulation puts a horseshoe vortex behind every bird and uses the Biot–Savart law to compute the upwash over the whole wing of every single bird, from all the other birds at once.

The best place to fly turns out to be with the centre about 0.9 span to the side of the bird ahead, so that the wingtips just overlap. Repeat that rule bird by bird to both sides, and you get a V. How far behind each other the birds fly matters less, because the upwash from a long vortex is nearly the same over several spans.

Why not a straight line?

In theory a flock flying side by side in a long line saves just as much. But then nobody can see the bird they have to keep station on. In a V each bird looks diagonally forward at its neighbour and can hold its place by eye. The formation is also robust: if one bird loses its place, only the birds behind it on the same leg suffer.

The leader is replaced

The bird in front has no bird ahead of it and flies in still air. It pays the full price. In northern bald ibises it has been measured that the birds take turns leading, and that two birds flying together keep a rough account of who has led longer. In the simulation the bird in front tires after 25–50 seconds. The more rested of the two birds behind it moves up, and the old leader slides out to the side and back to the end of the shorter leg.

Large flocks split up

Each bird keeps station on the bird ahead, but it reacts with a small delay. If the bird ahead goes off course, the one behind corrects too late and too much, and the error grows from bird to bird down the leg, like the concertina effect in a traffic queue. The longer the leg, the bigger the swings at the end. In the end a bird loses the upwash completely, and it and the birds behind it form their own smaller formation alongside. Try 61 birds at 50 % stability, and compare with 15 birds.

The same is true of small birds such as starlings, only much more so. Their vortices are weak compared with the disturbed air from their rapid wingbeats, so instead they fly in dense flocks without fixed order, as in a murmuration.

What the model computes

Each bird is a horseshoe vortex: a bound vortex along the wing with effective span πb/4 and two trailing vortices going back from the tips. The upwash is found at nine points along each wing, weighted by the elliptic lift distribution, and summed over all birds. The vortices have a core of 0.1 b so that the velocities do not become infinite.

The birds steer towards their place with a damped spring that reacts to where the bird ahead was a moment ago. The stability sets reaction time, precision and gusts. A bird that has been more than 1.5 spans from its place for more than 2.5 seconds breaks away.

The saving shown is for the induced power alone. The rest of a bird's power goes to drag on body and wings and is not affected by the upwash. The simulation is idealised: real vortices sink, wave with the wingbeat and decay, and birds do not fly precisely. Measured savings are therefore smaller, typically 10–15 % of the total power.

With phase-matched wingbeats, each bird flaps in step with the path of the wingtip of the bird ahead, as seen in northern bald ibises.

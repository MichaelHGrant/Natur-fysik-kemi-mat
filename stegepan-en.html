Why are hot pans ruined by cold water?

This phenomenon is called thermal shock. It happens because of extremely rapid and uneven cooling when the water hits the pan.

The mathematics behind the stress

1. Thermal linear expansion: $\Delta L = \alpha \cdot L_0 \cdot \Delta T$

2. Thermal stress: $\sigma = E \cdot \alpha \cdot \Delta T$

When the pan is cooled unevenly, mechanical stress ($\sigma$) arises. If this exceeds the material's yield strength (the limit at which it deforms permanently), the pan warps. For a brittle material such as cast iron it is instead the fracture strength that is exceeded, and the pan cracks.

The three factors in this simulator:

  • The material ($\alpha$ and $E$):
    - Aluminium has a high expansion coefficient ($\alpha$) and is soft. It expands a lot and bends extremely easily.
    - Stainless steel is strong and tough, and needs a larger temperature difference before it gives way.
    - Cast iron is extremely stiff, but brittle. Instead of warping, the internal stresses will often make it crack fatally.
  • The thickness (mechanical stiffness):
    A thick pan is far stiffer against bending: the bending stiffness grows with the cube of the thickness. It therefore resists warping, while a paper-thin pan has almost no resistance.
  • The diameter ($L_0$):
    The stress itself, $\sigma = E\alpha\Delta T$, does not depend on the size, but the contraction $\Delta L$ does. A wide 32 cm pan contracts twice as much as a small 16 cm pan, so the deflection becomes larger, and it is harder to cool the whole surface evenly. The bigger, the more vulnerable!

A year after the eclipse over northern Spain, the Moon's shadow returns — this time over the Strait of Gibraltar, North Africa and the Nile. At Luxor totality lasts 6 minutes and 23 seconds: the longest on easily accessible land this century. This page shows what the same eclipse looks like from eight different places.

Background

Why is this one so long?

Three things coincide

The Earth is near aphelion at the beginning of August, so the solar disc is at its smallest. The Moon passes perigee just 2½ hours before the eclipse and therefore appears unusually large. And the shadow strikes near the equator, where the Earth's surface moves fastest along with the shadow's own motion.

Together this gives an umbra up to 258 km wide and a totality of 6 m 23 s in the north-east of the New Valley in Egypt. The next time a total eclipse lasts longer on accessible land is in 2114.

Unlike 2026

The eclipse in August 2026 came at sunset with the Sun a few degrees above the horizon — you had to find a clear western view. This time the Sun is high in the morning: 39° above Málaga, 78° above Cairo. No hill, no house and no coastal haze will get in the way.

On the other hand the contrast is less dramatic. An eclipsed Sun at 40° altitude gives a strange, metallic dimming of the daylight rather than the long twilight colour seen over Cantabria.

From Denmark

Grenaa lies about 2,500 km north of the central line, and only a third of the solar disc is covered. That is too little to change the daylight noticeably — but plenty to see the bitten edge of the Sun through a filter, and to project the disc onto a piece of white paper.

The Sun is 44° above the south-east at maximum at 11:12. The whole sequence lasts 1 h 40 m.

Never look directly at the Sun

  • During all partial phases certified eye protection to ISO 12312-2 is required. Sunglasses, exposed film and smoked glass are no good.
  • Binoculars, telescope and camera must have the filter in front of the objective — never at the eyepiece end, where the heat is concentrated.
  • The retina has no sense of pain. The damage is not felt while it happens.
  • Only inside the path of totality — and only in the seconds when the solar disc is completely covered — can you look with the naked eye. All eight places on this page except Málaga require a filter throughout.
  • A pinhole camera or projection through binoculars onto white paper is the safest way to follow the sequence with a group.

Three photographs, a clock — and the whole orbit of Jupiter

In 1801 the 24-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss saved the newly discovered Ceres from vanishing into the glare of the Sun with only three observations. Here we apply his method to three eVscope2 images of Jupiter taken from Marshøj on Djursland — and recover the giant planet's orbit with an accuracy of about one part in a thousand.

I · The problem of 1801

When Ceres disappeared

On New Year's Day 1801 Giuseppe Piazzi in Palermo discovered a faint, wandering point of light — the first asteroid, Ceres. He managed to follow it for six weeks before it slipped into the glare of the Sun. The question now was: where will it reappear? The methods of the day required long series of observations; Piazzi's short arc of 9° seemed hopelessly inadequate.

Gauss attacked the problem in a completely new way. He realised that three determinations of direction — three pairs of right ascension and declination with their corresponding times — in principle contain exactly the six numbers that an orbit's six elements require. In December 1801 Franz von Zach found Ceres less than half a degree from Gauss's predicted position. The method, which he later published in Theoria Motus (1809), is at its core still used today when newly discovered asteroids and comets are to be given their first orbit.

Jupiter is of course no new discovery — but precisely for that reason it is a perfect test body: we know the answer and can check how well the method works on an arc that is only one and a half per cent of the 11.86-year orbit.

II · Datagrundlaget

Three evenings at the telescope

The three images were taken with a Unistellar eVscope2 from the observing site at Marshøj (56° N, 11° E). The images' timestamps give the observation times to the second — and time is the method's half of the measurement. The other half is the direction to the planet: right ascension \( \alpha \) and declination \( \delta \). In planet mode the eVscope images contain no background stars to plate-solve on, so the directions below are the astrometric positions belonging to the photographed times (corresponding to almanac or plate‑solve astrometry, corrected for light travel time). These are exactly the three pairs of numbers that Gauss's machinery has to be fed with.

III · Geometrien

Lines of sight from a moving platform

Each observation fixes a line of sight: a half-line from the Earth in the direction \( \hat{\boldsymbol\rho} \), given by

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\rho}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta\cos\alpha\\[2pt] \cos\delta\sin\alpha\\[2pt]\sin\delta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf r = \mathbf R + \rho\,\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}, \]

where \( \mathbf R \) is the Earth's heliocentric position vector (known from the almanac), \( \mathbf r \) the planet's unknown heliocentric position vector and \( \rho \) the unknown distance from the Earth to the planet along the line of sight. Three observations give three equations of this type — nine scalar equations with nine unknowns (\( \mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_3 \) bound together by the law of gravitation, and the three \( \rho_i \)).

IV · The method step by step

Gauss's machinery

1. Time intervals and Lagrange's f and g functions

With \( \tau_1=t_1-t_2 \), \( \tau_3=t_3-t_2 \) and \( \tau=\tau_3-\tau_1 \) (here \( \tau_1=-30.943 \) days, \( \tau_3=+36.025 \) days, \( \tau=66.968 \) days) Gauss exploits the fact that the three position vectors lie in the same orbital plane, so \( \mathbf r_1 \) and \( \mathbf r_3 \) can be written as combinations of \( \mathbf r_2 \) and \( \mathbf v_2 \):

\[ \mathbf r_{1,3}=f_{1,3}\,\mathbf r_2+g_{1,3}\,\mathbf v_2,\qquad f\simeq 1-\frac{\mu\,\tau^2}{2r_2^{\,3}},\qquad g\simeq \tau-\frac{\mu\,\tau^3}{6r_2^{\,3}}, \]

where the series expansions are initially truncated as shown. Herein hides the law of gravitation: \( \mu=GM_\odot=k^2 \) with Gauss's own constant \( k=0.01720209895 \) (units: au and days).

2. Scalar triple products and the eighth-degree equation

Inserting the f and g expressions into the line-of-sight equations and eliminating \( \rho_1,\rho_3 \) with scalar triple products of the unit vectors (Curtis' \( D \) determinants), one is left with one equation in one unknown — the planet's heliocentric distance at the middle observation:

\[ r_2^{\,8}+a\,r_2^{\,6}+b\,r_2^{\,3}+c=0 . \]

For our Jupiter data the coefficients become \( a=-27.5796 \), \( b=+38.4971 \), \( c=-13.6332 \) (powers of au), and the polynomial has three positive real roots:

The ambiguity is genuine and well known: three lines of sight alone cannot always distinguish a near, fast orbit from a distant, slow one. Here physics chooses for us — a body on an almost Earth-like orbit would move many degrees per day, not Jupiter's leisurely arcminutes.

3. From distance to state vector

With \( r_2=5.2259 \) au the three slant distances \( \rho_1,\rho_2,\rho_3 \) follow directly, hence \( \mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_3 \), and the velocity

\[ \mathbf v_2=\frac{-f_3\,\mathbf r_1+f_1\,\mathbf r_3}{f_1 g_3-f_3 g_1}. \]

Then we iterate: with \( (\mathbf r_2,\mathbf v_2) \) f and g are calculated exactly (universal variables instead of the truncated series), the slant distances are recalculated — and the light travel time \( \rho_i/c \) (33–41 minutes!) is subtracted from the observation times. The process here converges in 6 iterations to

\[ \mathbf r_2=(-2.0126,\;+4.8221,\;+0.0250)\ \mathrm{au},\qquad |\mathbf v_2| = 13.01\ \mathrm{km/s}. \]

4. Baneelementerne

The state vector is translated into Kepler elements via the angular momentum \( \mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v \), the eccentricity vector \( \mathbf e=(\mathbf v\times\mathbf h)/\mu-\hat{\mathbf r} \) and the energy \( \varepsilon=v^2/2-\mu/r=-\mu/2a \). With an arc of just 1.5 % of the orbit, \( a \), \( e \), \( i \) and \( \Omega \) are hit at the level of parts per thousand. Only the longitude of perihelion \( \varpi \) is off by a degree or so — just as expected: with \( e\approx 0.05 \) the orbit is so nearly circular that the direction of perihelion is the most poorly determined quantity. That, incidentally, was the same thing Gauss experienced with Ceres.

Why does it work so well?

Two gifts from geometry: first, the first image was taken four days after opposition, when the parallax from the Earth's own motion is greatest — the lines of sight »scissor« strongly and fix the distance sharply. Second, 67 days is enough for Jupiter's own curvature of the orbit to be felt in the third-order terms of the f and g series. Gauss's method is built precisely for this middleweight: arcs too short for classical orbit fitting, but long enough for the curvature to speak.

Caveats that belong here

The calculation is geocentric: from 56° N topocentric parallax shifts Jupiter by less than 2 arcseconds, which is of no consequence here. The light travel time, on the other hand, is not negligible (up to 41 minutes) and is included in the iteration. The observation times are read from the images' timestamps (CET = UT+1); the positions are astrometric J2000 quantities belonging to exactly these times. If you want to make the exercise completely »raw«, you photograph the planet in wide-field mode with background stars and plate-solve yourself — the method downstream is exactly the same.

VI · Perspective

From Palermo 1801 to Marshøj 2026

The astonishing thing about Gauss's method is its economy: six measured numbers (three pairs of angles) plus three readings of a clock determine all six orbital elements — because Newton's law of gravitation supplies all the missing information. Every time an amateur astronomer today reports astrometry of a newly found comet to the Minor Planet Center, it is this machine (in modernised form) that spins at the other end.

And so the circle is closed: a telescope in a field on Djursland, three clear evenings over a winter, and mathematics from 1801 — that is all it takes to weigh the solar system's largest planet into its place.

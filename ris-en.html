The physics behind the realistic steam holes

In nature, systems try to minimise their energy. When many steam holes have to share a limited area (such as the surface in a pot), they would ideally arrange themselves in a perfect hexagonal pattern in order to be as far apart from one another as possible (maximising flow, minimising pressure).

Why is the pattern NOT perfectly hexagonal in reality?

There are two main physical reasons why the pattern does not turn out as a flawless lattice and instead becomes "imperfectly close-packed":

  1. Granular heterogeneity (structural noise): Rice is not a uniform liquid. It consists of solid grains of different sizes lying at random. The local permeability (how easily the steam gets through) varies from millimetre to millimetre. The steam finds the *locally* easiest route, which shifts the hole away from the perfect mathematical position.
  2. Boundary conditions (geometric frustration): Mathematically, a round pot cannot be filled with perfect hexagons without creating "topological defects" (faults in the lattice) near the edge. The shape of the edge forces the pattern to deform.

The mathematics behind the deviation

We can model the position of a hole, $\vec{r}_i$, as a combination of its ideal position in a crystal lattice $\vec{r}_{ideal}$ plus a noise term $\vec{\eta}$ that represents the granular disorder:

$$ \vec{r}_i = \vec{r}_{ideal} + \vec{\eta}(x, y) $$
$$ \vec{\eta} \propto \nabla \kappa(x,y) $$

Where:
$\vec{r}_i$ = The actual position of the steam hole on the surface.
$\vec{r}_{ideal}$ = The perfect hexagonal position determined by the mean spacing $\lambda$.
$\vec{\eta}(x,y)$ = The displacement vector (the noise) created by local conditions.
$\kappa(x,y)$ = The local field of the permeability of the rice mass. The hole is drawn to where the resistance is lowest.

In the animation behind this text, this is implemented by first calculating a hexagonal close-packing lattice, after which each node is moved randomly in an arbitrary direction by a magnitude corresponding to about 40% of the spacing between the holes. This creates the familiar, natural rice pattern.

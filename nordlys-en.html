Northern and southern lights: when the solar wind makes the sky glow

Electrons from the Earth's magnetic tail plunge into the atmosphere along the magnetic field and make oxygen and nitrogen glow. It happens at the same time round both magnetic poles, in two ovals that stay fixed relative to the Sun while the Earth turns underneath them. Turn the Kp index up, set off a substorm, and see the sky from Denmark, Tromsø or Tasmania. Drag the Earth to turn it.

At Kp 0–2 the oval lies far to the north over Iceland and northern Norway. At Kp 4–5 the aurora can be seen above the northern horizon from Denmark, and at Kp 7–9 it can stand overhead. The oval is fixed relative to the Sun: the strongest activity is on the night side a little before midnight.

The sky seen from Djursland

From the Sun to the sky

The Sun constantly sends out the solar wind: a thin plasma of protons and electrons that moves at 300–800 km/s and carries the Sun's magnetic field with it. The Earth's magnetic field keeps the solar wind out and forms the magnetosphere, a cavity squeezed to about 10 Earth radii on the day side and drawn out into a long tail on the night side. Switch on Magnetopause and watch it being pushed in as Kp rises.

When the solar wind's magnetic field points south, it can connect to the Earth's field on the day side. The field lines are carried over the poles and stored in the tail, and energy builds up. Suddenly the field reconnects in the tail, and plasma is shot towards the Earth. This is a substorm. Electrons are accelerated to a few thousand electronvolts along the field lines and plunge into the atmosphere in a band round the magnetic pole: the auroral oval. Press Set off a substorm and watch the oval brighten and spread polewards around midnight.

Why an oval round the magnetic pole

In a dipole field a field line follows the curve

r = L · RE · cos²λ

where λ is the magnetic latitude and L is the distance of the field line from the Earth's centre at the equator, in Earth radii. The field line meets the ground at the latitude Λ with cos²Λ = 1/L. The plasma in the tail lies around L = 6–10, which gives Λ ≈ 66–72°. That is why the aurora is a ring round the magnetic pole, not a disc over it. The geomagnetic north pole lies in north-west Greenland, so the oval lies further south over North America than over Europe. Djursland lies at magnetic latitude 56°, on the field line L ≈ 3.2. Only when the oval spreads towards the equator at high Kp does it come within sight.

Particles in a magnetic field

The Lorentz force F = qv × B makes a charged particle spiral round the field line. Where the field gets stronger, near the poles, the motion along the field is slowed down and the particle turns back. This is called a magnetic mirror. A particle with pitch angle α at the equator turns back where

sin²α = Beq / Bmirror

Only particles with a small pitch angle, inside the loss cone, reach the atmosphere before they turn. At L = 6 the loss cone is under 3°. The particles in the radiation belts, by contrast, are trapped, bounce back and forth between the hemispheres and drift round the Earth: electrons to the east, protons to the west. Switch on Magnetic field and particles. The electrons are blue, the protons orange, and now and then an electron is sent into the loss cone. It hits the atmosphere in the oval and gives a flash of light.

In the model the spirals are made many thousands of times larger than they are. An electron of 5 keV has a gyroradius of about 2 km at L = 6 and a few centimetres at 100 km height, where it circles 1.4 million times a second.

The colours: oxygen and nitrogen

The electrons collide with atoms and molecules and excite them. The light comes when they fall back to the ground state. The colour depends on which atom glows, and at what height:

The red oxygen line comes from a state that lives for 110 seconds. Below about 200 km the atom hits a molecule long before that, and the energy is lost without light. The red colour is therefore only seen high up, where the air is thin. The green line lives only 0.7 seconds and survives down to about 100 km. The more energy the electrons have, the deeper they penetrate, and the lower the lower edge lies. Try the Electron energy slider: soft electrons below 1 keV give mostly red aurora, hard electrons of 10–30 keV give a sharp, pink lower edge.

Why the aurora is red in Denmark

From a height h you can see out to the horizon distance d ≈ √(2REh). The green part at 110 km height can be seen up to about 1200 km away, but the red top at 250 km height can be seen up to 1800 km away. When the oval lies over southern Norway and Scotland, from Denmark you see only the top of the aurora, low above the northern horizon, and it is red. Cameras see the colour better than the eye, because the eye's colour vision is weak in the dark. That is how many Danes saw the aurora during the great storm of May 2024.

Northern and southern lights at once

The two ovals are linked by the same field lines, so the northern and southern lights occur at the same time. Even so, they are not mirror images. The solar wind's magnetic field has an east–west component that twists the ovals in opposite directions, and the hemisphere in summer has a more conducting upper atmosphere. The southern lights are seen from Tasmania, southern New Zealand, the tip of South America and Antarctica. Choose Hobart or Dunedin and look south.

The Kp index

Kp runs from 0 to 9 and measures how much the Earth's magnetic field is disturbed over three hours, from magnetic observatories at mid-latitudes. The scale is nearly logarithmic. Kp 5 is a minor geomagnetic storm, Kp 9 an extreme one. The Fetch current Kp button fetches the latest estimate from NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center.

What the model computes

The Earth's field is a dipole at the Earth's centre, tilted towards the geomagnetic pole. The direction of the Sun is computed from the date and time. The boundaries of the oval are a simple fit after Feldstein and Starkov as a function of Kp and magnetic local time. The height and colours of the aurora follow the electron energy and the four emissions in the table. The particle paths are computed with the Boris method in the dipole field, with an enlarged gyroradius. The magnetopause follows Shue et al.

The sky is computed geometrically: each piece of the aurora is drawn in the direction and at the height above the horizon where it stands seen from the place, brighter the closer it is.

Limitations

The real magnetic field is not a pure dipole, so the position of the oval over a particular place can differ by a couple of degrees from the model's. The edge of the oval at midnight follows NOAA's rule of thumb for Kp and geomagnetic latitude. The outermost edge is often so faint that the eye does not see it. The oval is an average without the solar wind's magnetic field, and the arcs, rays and folds are drawn with random noise, not computed.

History

Around 1900 the Norwegian physicist Kristian Birkeland fired electrons at a magnetised sphere, a terrella, in a vacuum chamber and saw rings of light round the poles. The Dane Sophus Tromholt studied the aurora in Kautokeino during the first International Polar Year, 1882–83. The auroral oval was mapped in the 1960s, when pictures from cameras across the Arctic were put together.

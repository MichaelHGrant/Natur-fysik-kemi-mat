The category mistake of the New Atheism

Why Dawkins, Hitchens and Harris miss the God that classical theology speaks of — and why the real questions are still waiting, honestly posed.

Michael Grant  ·  michael.h.grant@gmail.com

In the course of a single decade from the mid-2000s, four books landed with a bang in public debate. Richard Dawkins' The God Delusion, Christopher Hitchens' God Is Not Great, Sam Harris' The End of Faith and Daniel Dennett's Breaking the Spell turned atheism into bestseller material and their authors into »the four horsemen«. The message was simple and self-assured: God is a hypothesis about reality, science has tested it, and it does not hold. I will try to show here that the horsemen's central move rests on a confusion so fundamental that much of their ammunition misses the mark — but also, with equal seriousness, that some of their challenges are real and deserve an honest answer, not a cheap dismissal.

The claim

The four horsemen

Let us begin by granting the opponent his due where he is right — otherwise an answer is worth nothing. The new atheists are onto something real when they point out that many popular arguments for God are weak, that religious institutions have done immense harm, and that a faith which demands to be placed above reason is no faith worth having. On all three points they have a point, and a Christian thinker should say so without blinking.

But behind the polemic lies a particular philosophical assumption, and it is the one on which everything stands or falls. The assumption is that the word »God« denotes an object among objects — a very large, very powerful being which either exists or does not exist within the same reality as stars, galaxies and bacteria. God becomes a kind of cosmic engineer, a hypothesis on a par with other hypotheses, which science can therefore settle. From precisely that assumption the whole arsenal follows: Dawkins' famous question of who, then, created the creator; Bertrand Russell's teapot, floating invisibly between the Earth and Mars and impossible to disprove; the Flying Spaghetti Monster; the demand for »evidence« for God of the same kind we demand for a new elementary particle.

All these objections are sharp and witty. And they are all aimed at a God whom classical theology has never proposed.

The mistake

The box God never lay in

The philosopher Gilbert Ryle once told of a visitor who was shown round the university — the colleges, the libraries, the laboratories — and finally asked: »But where is the university itself?« The visitor had taken »the university« to be yet another building alongside the others, whereas in reality it was simply the way in which everything already seen was organised. The question was not hard to answer. It was wrongly posed. Ryle called this sort of thing a category mistake: treating something that belongs to one kind as if it belonged to another.

The classical doctrine of God — in Thomas Aquinas, and in our own time in thinkers such as Herbert McCabe, Brian Davies and David Bentley Hart — makes its main claim precisely to avoid this mistake. God is not a being, it maintains: not one entry in the inventory of existing things, however great and powerful it might be. God is not the largest object in or behind the universe, but ipsum esse subsistens — the very act of being, the reason there is anything at all rather than nothing. God is not a thing in the world; God is the reason there is a world.

You cannot disprove a God you have never met — and the question »who created God?« already presupposes the mistake it thinks it exposes.

If this is right, the horsemen's best ammunition falls away on the spot. »Who created the creator?« only bites if God is himself a created sort of thing within the chain of causes — but that is precisely what the doctrine denies. To compare God with a teapot or a spaghetti monster already presupposes the confusion, for a teapot is an object in the world, whereas what is meant by God is that which sustains there being a world to have objects in. »Where is God?« and »what caused God?« then land as malformed — rather like the question »what lies north of the North Pole?« The words are grammatically flawless; what they point to simply ceases to make sense. The atheist and the believer who quarrel about this God are often quarrelling about the same fictitious object — and have both misunderstood the matter.

The explanation

Two kinds of explanation

From this follows a fixed point where science and faith are set up as competitors. To treat a scientific and a religious explanation as rival answers to the same question is as a rule a category mistake. The physics of heat transfer and »I fancied a cup of tea« both explain why the kettle is boiling — but they belong to different orders of explanation, not to one and the same contest. The one answer does not make the other superfluous. When Laplace, so the story goes, explained his system of the world to Napoleon without mentioning God, and the emperor asked about the omission, he is said to have replied: »Sire, je n'avais pas besoin de cette hypothèse« — I had no need of that hypothesis. And on the classical understanding that is perfectly fine: God was never a term in the equations of celestial mechanics. To say that physics has made God superfluous is like saying that grammar has made Shakespeare superfluous.

This mistake, however, cuts both ways, and here the believer must sweep his own doorstep. The »God of the gaps« — the god one inserts as an extra efficient cause to close a gap physics has not yet filled — makes exactly the same confusion, only from the other side. Georges Lemaître, the priest and physicist who first proposed what we call the Big Bang, understood this precisely and warned against turning his cosmology into a proof of creation: the physical beginning of the universe and its metaphysical dependence on God are simply different questions. Augustine had said something related a millennium and a half earlier: asked what God was doing before he created the world, he answered that time itself is part of creation, so there is no »before« for the deed to fill. A God who is only used to plug the gaps of science shrinks each time a gap is closed. The God faith speaks of was never in any gap.

The mirror

A mirror image

Here lies the deepest and most surprising point, which David Bentley Hart in particular has sharpened: the New Atheism tacitly shares the metaphysics it believes it is doing away with. It presupposes that »to exist« means exactly the same for God as for a stone — that there is one common stock of existing things, in which God, if he existed, would be the very largest specimen. But if one searches the stock and does not find him, one concludes that he is not there. It is an astonishingly literal notion, and it is the mirror image of fundamentalism. The New Atheism reads the sacred texts just as crudely literally as the literalists it mocks, and it poses the question »does God exist?« as if it were of the same kind as »does the Loch Ness monster exist?«

The long, subtle tradition — Maimonides, Pseudo-Dionysius, Aquinas, the negative theology which insists that our language about God is analogical and not literal — is not engaged at all. The philosopher Alvin Plantinga remarked drily that Dawkins' arguments would hardly pass an introductory philosophy exam. The literary critic Terry Eagleton, himself no pious man, joked that »Ditchkins« — his amalgam of Dawkins and Hitchens — bought his theology on sale. And it is worth remembering that Antony Flew, one of the twentieth century's most prominent atheist philosophers, late in life followed the arguments where they led him, and ended up a deist. That shows at the very least that the questions are not as settled as the horsemen lead their readers to believe.

The other mistake

The congruence mistake — when similarity is taken for identity

The category mistake, however, is not the only confusion at play, and the other one is more refined. Geometry, as every schoolchild meets it, supplies both the name and the distinction: two triangles are similar when all the angles agree and all the ratios of the sides are preserved — and they are congruent when in addition they are exactly the same size, so that one covers the other completely. Similarity preserves the whole structure and is nevertheless not identity. A congruence mistake is then to take the first for the second: the right box, a genuine structural likeness — but a false claim of exact coincidence, term by term.

The category mistake says: the box is wrongly labelled. The congruence mistake says: the box is right — but the contents do not match term by term.

It is precisely this mistake that the classical doctrine of analogy guards against. To say »God is good« is to claim a real likeness — but not that the word »good« means exactly the same of God as of a human being, only in a larger format; that would put God and creature in the same common genus. The Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 wrote the limit into the doctrine itself: however great a likeness may be pointed out between the Creator and the creature, an even greater unlikeness must be noted. It is — seven centuries before Ryle — a formal declaration of an analogy's domain of validity. And Duns Scotus, who defended univocity, must be given his due: his claim applied only to a thin common core, the very concept of »being«, precisely so that inferences about God would be possible at all. The dispute between him and Thomas is at bottom a dispute about where the congruence ends — and that both knew that this very question was decisive is the whole point.

With that tool in hand one can distinguish more sharply than the category mistake alone allows. Read congruently — as if God were a being with limbs and moods — »the hand of God« and »God repented« are plain mistakes, and the New Atheism has an easy time. Read as images — imago, not congruence — they bear weight. And perhaps Dawkins' God is a congruence mistake before it is a category mistake: the tacit assumption that »exists« means exactly the same said of the things in the world and of the ground of the world — that existence maps congruently from teapots to God.

Natural science, incidentally, lives by the same discipline. Maxwell deliberately rode on analogies — field lines as streamlines in an imagined fluid — all the way to the equations, and dismounted before the likeness was turned into identity; the caloric theory, by contrast, fell precisely because it took heat's likeness in flow to a fluid for an identity with a substance. Mathematics even has a measure for the matter: a structure-preserving map that loses nothing is called an isomorphism; one that loses something, a homomorphism — and the loss is measured by its kernel. Every analogy is a homomorphism; the congruence mistake is to forget that it has a kernel. Mature thinking — in physics as in theology — therefore does not ask whether a likeness is »true«, but how far it reaches, and what limits it.

The two mistakes really belong on one and the same scale, which measures how much structure a map preserves: nothing at one extreme — the land of the category mistake, where the predicate finds no purchase at all — and everything, within a stated domain, at the other. The category mistake is in that sense the limiting case of the congruence mistake: the map whose kernel is the whole. It follows that they each require their own treatment. A category mistake must be dissolved — the question is withdrawn and retired. A congruence mistake must be surveyed — the likeness is genuine, and the task is to find its limit. And the two can breed each other. If an image is pressed beyond its domain, it produces wrongly posed questions on a conveyor belt: »what was God doing before he created the world?« is precisely the child of a congruent reading of creation as an act in time — and Augustine's answer was, as we saw, a declaration of domain: time itself belongs to what is created. Conversely, a too hasty category diagnosis can hide a genuine likeness; whoever, with the positivists, dismisses all talk of God as meaningless throws out the load-bearing bridge of analogy along with its misuse.

The objection

Where they hit something real

Now one might be tempted to let the matter rest here, content with having exposed the confusion. But that would be to make the opposite mistake — to wave away every single atheist objection as if it were merely a muddle. It is not. Some of the strongest arguments against faith are not category mistakes, and honesty requires that one says so out loud.

The problem of evil is the foremost. Why does a God who is said to be both almighty and perfectly good permit the amount of meaningless suffering the world contains? That question is not dissolved by any distinction between orders of explanation; it is real, it is heavy, and it has rightly tormented believing minds for millennia. Brian Davies and others have written acutely about it, but no one should pretend it is easy. The same applies to the hiddenness of God — the philosopher J.L. Schellenberg's objection that if a loving God existed, would sincere doubt then be so widespread? These are real questions which deserve real answers, not rhetorical fencing. A believer who only scores debating points has misunderstood the seriousness of the challenge just as deeply as the atheist misunderstands what the word »God« means.

The discipline

Knowing when a question is real

There is a lesson here which reaches beyond the subject of the dispute, and it is slightly self-critical. The category mistake is a powerful tool — it clears away the ghost in the machine, the God of the gaps, the question of what lies north of the North Pole. But precisely its satisfying force makes it dangerous, for »it's just a category mistake« can be used to wave away problems that are hard rather than confused.

Physics knows the temptation better than most. It is tempting to dismiss Schrödinger's cat — the one said to be both alive and dead — as mere loose talk. But beneath the apparently paradoxical formulation lies the measurement problem: a real, unsolved question about how definite outcomes arise at all, which no clever distinction can dissolve. Even the most seductive way out — reading the wave function as »just« a probability distribution in which the cat is one of the two, and we merely do not know which — is at bottom a congruence mistake: the likeness is genuine, but the kernel of the map is the interference terms, precisely the terms that draw the fringe patterns of quantum mechanics and which no distribution of ignorance possesses. The PBR theorem from 2012 can even be read as the declaration of domain for that map. The same discipline applies here. »Science has disproved God« hits too hard in one direction; but »the atheist has no real arguments« hits just as hard in the other. The mature thinker learns to distinguish the wrongly posed question from the genuinely hard one — on both sides of the table. It is easy to spot the confusion in one's opponent; the art is to spot when one is using it oneself to avoid thinking.

It is easy to spot the category mistake in one's opponent. The art is to know when one is using it oneself to get round something real.

The questions

The questions none of us escape

Why is there anything at all rather than nothing? Why is the world intelligible — why does mathematics grip reality as if it were written for it? What sustains consciousness, the good, the very fact of existing? These are not scientific questions, and they are not pious evasions. They are the oldest questions humanity has, and they still stand open. The real weakness of the New Atheism is not that it poses them wrongly — we all do that now and then — but that it is so sure they have been answered and closed. That is precisely the claim the facts do not bear out.

One does not need to share my faith to see this. One need only insist that the conversation is conducted at its own level: that one disproves the God who is actually claimed, and not a caricature; that one distinguishes what science can settle from what it does not touch at all; and that one meets the real objections — the problem of evil, the hiddenness of God — with the seriousness they deserve. If one does that, one discovers that the question of God is not a remnant of superstition that science has swept off the table, but one of the very few truly great conversations humanity has had — and is still having.

Revelation

»I Am« — from reason to revelation

All this is what reason can reach — and it is not little. With reason alone a human being can come to the knowledge that God is: that behind all that exists there is a ground, being itself. But there is also revealed truth, and here the path of reason ends while something else begins. To know not merely that God is, but who he is — so that there can be a meeting and not merely an inference — God himself must speak. Christianity's claim is that he has done so.

The name God gives when Moses asks who he is, is astonishing — for it is precisely what the philosophers were groping for:

Exodus 3:14

God said to Moses: »I am who I am.« And he said: »Say this to the people of Israel: I Am has sent me to you.«

Not a being among beings, not a thing with a name like other things, but being itself: I Am. What Thomas Aquinas called ipsum esse subsistens is already here — said by God of himself. The metaphysical insight and the revealed name are one and the same.

And then the decisive point. God did not reveal himself in a book, not in a political or philosophical manifesto, but directly and personally. He stepped into his own creation — into time and space, into the history of humanity — as one of us, in the man Jesus. When Jesus took the same divine name on his own lips, his listeners understood him perfectly well:

John 8:58

Jesus said to them: »Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am.«

It was no pious figure of speech. It was the claim to be the one who spoke to Moses from the burning bush. Paul captures the shape of this self-emptying in what is perhaps Christianity's oldest hymn:

Philippians 2:5–11

Have this mind among yourselves, which was in Christ Jesus,
who, though he was in the form of God,
did not count equality with God
a thing to be grasped,
but emptied himself,
taking the form of a servant,
being born in the likeness of men.
And being found in human form,
he humbled himself
and became obedient unto death,
even death on a cross.
Therefore God has highly exalted him
and bestowed on him the name which is above every name,
that at the name of Jesus
every knee should bow,
in heaven and on earth and under the earth,
and every tongue confess
that Jesus Christ is Lord,
to the glory of God the Father.

Here the matter reaches further than any argument can carry. Reason can clear the ground and point; but the one who steps out onto it is a person, not a conclusion. That is the difference between inferring that there must be a God — and hearing him say: I Am.

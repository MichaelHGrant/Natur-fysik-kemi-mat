i.

The difference in gravity — not its size

Newton's universal gravitation says that the Moon pulls on every cubic centimetre of the Earth. But the pull is not the same everywhere. The near side lies ~12,700 km closer to the Moon than the far side, and because gravity decreases with the square of the distance, the difference — the tidal force — is proportional to 1/r³.

The starting point: Newton's law of gravitation

Newton's universal law of gravitation (1687)
$$F_g = \frac{G \, M \, m}{r^2}$$
Fgthe gravitational force between two bodies (N) Gthe gravitational constant = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² Mthe mass of the attracting body (kg) — e.g. the Moon mthe mass of the test mass (kg) — e.g. 1 kg of water rdistance between the centres of the bodies (m)
Note the r² in the denominator: if you double the distance, the force falls to a quarter. It is this dependence on distance that is at the core of all tidal mechanics.

Derivation of the tidal force

The tidal force is not gravity itself — it is the difference between the pull on the Earth's near side and the pull on the Earth's centre. Let us derive the formula step by step.

Step 1 · Force on the Earth's centre
$$F_0 = \frac{G \, M \, m}{r^2}$$
Step 2 · Force on a point on the near side
$$F_{near} = \frac{G \, M \, m}{(r-R)^2}$$
The point is R closer to the Moon, so the distance is r − R.
Step 3 · The difference
$$\Delta F = F_{near} - F_0 = G \, M \, m \left[ \frac{1}{(r-R)^2} - \frac{1}{r^2} \right]$$
Step 4 · Taylor expansion (R ≪ r)
$$\frac{1}{(r-R)^2} = \frac{1}{r^2}\left(1-\frac{R}{r}\right)^{-2} \approx \frac{1}{r^2}\left(1 + \frac{2R}{r}\right)$$
Since R/r ≈ 0.017 (very small), we can use a first-order Taylor expansion: (1−x)⁻² ≈ 1 + 2x.
Step 5 · The tidal force
$$\boxed{\,F_{tidal} \approx \frac{2 \, G \, M \, m \, R}{r^3}\,}$$
The result: a force that falls off with r³, not r². This is why the nearby Moon dominates over the distant Sun — even though the Sun is enormously more massive.

The Moon's tidal force on 1 kg at the Earth's surface

We insert all the numbers into the formula F = 2GMmR/r³ with m = 1 kg, M = M☾, r = r☾
1.Write the equation with numbers
$$F_{☾} = \frac{2 \cdot (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \cdot (7.342 \times 10^{22}) \cdot (6.371 \times 10^{6}) \cdot 1}{(3.844 \times 10^{8})^3}$$
2.Calculate the numerator
$$2 \cdot 6.674 \cdot 7.342 \cdot 6.371 = 624.3$$

Potenser: 10⁻¹¹ · 10²² · 10⁶ = 10¹⁷

$$\text{Numerator} = 624.3 \times 10^{17} = 6.243 \times 10^{19}$$
3.Calculate the denominator
$$(3.844)^3 = 56.81 \quad \Rightarrow \quad (3.844 \times 10^{8})^3 = 5.681 \times 10^{25}$$
4.Divider
$$F_{☾} = \frac{6.243 \times 10^{19}}{5.681 \times 10^{25}} = \class{answer}{1.10 \times 10^{-6} \text{ N}}$$
Result
Fmoon ≈ 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ N per kg

For comparison: the Earth's own gravity on 1 kg is 9.81 N. The tidal force is thus only 1 part in ~9 million of gravity — infinitely weak in absolute terms, but enough to move oceans all over the globe.

The Sun's tidal force on the same kilogram

The Sun is ~27 million times more massive than the Moon, but lies ~390 times further away. Let us see which wins.
1.Insert values
$$F_{☉} = \frac{2 \cdot (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) \cdot (1.989 \times 10^{30}) \cdot (6.371 \times 10^{6}) \cdot 1}{(1.496 \times 10^{11})^3}$$
2.Numerator
$$2 \cdot 6.674 \cdot 1.989 \cdot 6.371 = 169.1$$ $$\text{Numerator} = 169.1 \times 10^{25} = 1.691 \times 10^{27}$$
3.Denominator
$$(1.496)^3 = 3.348 \quad \Rightarrow \quad (1.496 \times 10^{11})^3 = 3.348 \times 10^{33}$$
4.Divider
$$F_{☉} = \frac{1.691 \times 10^{27}}{3.348 \times 10^{33}} = \class{answer}{5.05 \times 10^{-7} \text{ N}}$$
5.The ratio between them
$$\frac{F_{☉}}{F_{☾}} = \frac{5.05 \times 10^{-7}}{1.10 \times 10^{-6}} = \class{answer}{0.459}$$
Result
Fsun ≈ 0.46 · Fmoon

The Sun contributes ~46% of the Moon's tidal force. Although its mass is astronomically greater, it loses to the much closer Moon — because r³ penalises distance hard. This number — 0.46 — is the cornerstone of the difference between spring tide and neap tide.

How high does the bulge get?

If the Earth were covered by a global ocean without continents, the equilibrium height of the tidal bulge would be:

Equilibrium bulge height
$$h = \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{M}{M_\oplus} \cdot \left(\frac{R_\oplus}{r}\right)^3 \cdot R_\oplus$$
Derived from the fact that the water surface follows a line of constant gravitational potential. The factor 3/2 comes from the geometry of the two bulges.

The bulge heights of the Moon and the Sun

We insert directly with the same constants as above.
1.The Moon's bulge
$$h_{☾} = \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{7.342 \times 10^{22}}{5.972 \times 10^{24}} \cdot \left(\frac{6.371 \times 10^{6}}{3.844 \times 10^{8}}\right)^3 \cdot (6.371 \times 10^{6})$$ $$h_{☾} = 1.5 \cdot 0.01230 \cdot 4.56 \times 10^{-6} \cdot 6.371 \times 10^{6}$$ $$h_{☾} \approx \class{answer}{0.54 \text{ m} = 54 \text{ cm}}$$
2.The Sun's bulge
$$h_{☉} = \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{1.989 \times 10^{30}}{5.972 \times 10^{24}} \cdot \left(\frac{6.371 \times 10^{6}}{1.496 \times 10^{11}}\right)^3 \cdot (6.371 \times 10^{6})$$ $$h_{☉} \approx \class{answer}{0.25 \text{ m} = 25 \text{ cm}}$$
3.Springflod & niptid

When the Sun and Moon are in line, the bulges add up. When they are at 90°, they subtract:

$$h_{spring} = h_{☾} + h_{☉} \approx 54 + 25 = \class{answer}{79 \text{ cm}}$$ $$h_{nip} = h_{☾} - h_{☉} \approx 54 - 25 = \class{answer}{29 \text{ cm}}$$
Result — comparison
Springflod / niptid ≈ 2,7 ×

Spring tide is thus theoretically about 2.7 times higher than neap tide. In reality these values are dramatically amplified by local geography — continental shelves, fjords and resonance in ocean basins can raise tides to 16 metres in the Bay of Fundy. But the underlying physical scale is set by these simple calculations.

ii.

The bulge that runs ahead

In an idealised universe without friction, the water bulges would lie exactly in line with the Moon. But the Earth is not ideal — it rotates eastwards once a day, while the Moon only moves once around its orbit in 27.3 days. The Earth thus rotates ~27 times faster than the Moon orbits.

Friction between seawater and the seabed (especially over the continental shelves) therefore drags the bulges along with the Earth's rotation — ahead of the Moon, not behind it. The leading bulge exerts a torque that: (a) slows the Earth's rotation (~1.7 ms per century) and (b) accelerates the Moon in its orbit, so that it drifts away from us by ~3.8 cm per year.

iii.

An inclined orbit, a cooperating sun

The Moon's orbit does not lie in the Earth's equatorial plane, nor exactly in the ecliptic. It is inclined 5.14° to the ecliptic — that is why the Moon does not pass across the Sun's disc every month, and solar eclipses are rare. Add the Earth's own axial tilt of 23.5°, and the result is a tidal pattern that varies in complex ways through the year.

When the Sun, Earth and Moon are in line (new moon or full moon) their tidal forces add up — spring tide. When they are at 90° (first and third quarter) they work against each other — neap tide.

iv.

Why the Moon never turns its back

The same mechanism that slows the Earth's rotation stopped the Moon's long ago. Early in the history of the solar system the Moon rotated faster than it orbited. The Earth's gravity created bulges in the Moon's bedrock, and friction from these bulges slowed the Moon's spin over millions of years, until rotation = orbital period: exactly 27.3 days. The result is called synchronous rotation or tidal locking.

v.

Galileo's blind spot, Newton's breakthrough

In the 17th century tides were one of the most pressing unsolved problems in physics. Everyone could see them. No one could explain them. Galileo Galilei believed he had the solution — and he was wrong. His younger contemporary, Johannes Kepler, came closer to the truth but was rejected. It was Isaac Newton, half a century after Galileo's death, who put things right.

G
Galileo Galilei
1564 — 1642

In the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632) Galileo used the tides as proof of the Earth's motion — the seas were, he claimed, water "sloshing" in basins (the ocean basins) because of the combination of the Earth's daily rotation and annual orbit around the Sun.

He explicitly rejected that the Moon could be the cause, and called this idea "childishly useless". The idea of "occult forces" acting at a distance was to him unscientific.

Why he was wrong: his theory predicted only one high tide per day — reality shows two. It could not explain why the tides follow the Moon's position. And physically the mechanism did not work.

"The tides are the proof that the Earth moves"
— an elegant argument, based on the wrong mechanics.
N
Isaac Newton
1643 — 1727

In the Principia Mathematica (1687), Book III, Newton showed that universal gravitation — the same force that makes apples fall — extended to the Moon and the Sun, and that the difference in their gravity across the Earth's diameter produced the tidal bulges.

He derived that two bulges — one towards the Moon, one away from the Moon — arose naturally from his mathematics. He predicted spring and neap tides quantitatively, and calculated the ratio between solar and lunar tides.

Why he was right: his theory predicted two daily high tides, the correct correlation with the Moon's position, a ratio between solar and lunar tides of the right order of magnitude (Newton's own figure was too large, because he overestimated the Moon's mass) — all from a single equation.

"Hypotheses non fingo"
— I frame no hypotheses. The mathematics spoke.
Galileo saw the tides as the stamp of rotation.
Newton saw them as the fingerprint of gravity. — the difference between genius and breakthrough

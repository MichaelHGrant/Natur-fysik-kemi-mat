An information paradigm · 1926 – 2026

Can my cat be both alive and dead at the same time?

100 years after Schrödinger's equation we revisit not just the equation itself, but the bold ideas that made it possible.

Michael Grant

ψ

While the box is closed: a superposition – not of the cat, but of your knowledge of it.

In January 1926 – 100 years ago – Annalen der Physik received a paper with the sober title Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem. The author was the 38-year-old Erwin Schrödinger, and the paper contained what today stands as perhaps the most beautiful equation in modern physics. The centenary is a good occasion to revisit not just the equation itself, but the series of bold ideas that made it possible – and to ask ourselves whether we give our students the opportunity to let their imagination work with the same freedom.

01 ·The atom that could not last

Rutherford's scattering experiment of 1911 left physics with a paradox that classical electrodynamics could not handle. An accelerated charge emits radiation, and since electrons in circular orbits are accelerated charges, the atom ought to collapse in the order of \(10^{-11}\) seconds. But atoms stubbornly go on existing – after all, we are still here.

Bohr's solution of 1913 was almost Dadaist in its radicalism: let us simply forbid all orbits except those in which the angular momentum is an integer multiple of \(\hbar\):

Bohrs kvantiseringsregel \[ m v r = n\hbar \]

Bohr postulated a rule that worked because it gave the right spectral lines for hydrogen, and left the rest to posterity.

02 ·De Broglie's symmetry argument

Where Bohr needed an ad hoc rule, Louis de Broglie supplied its physical interpretation in 1924. His doctoral thesis contained one of the most elegant symmetry arguments: if light waves carry momentum \(p = h/\lambda\), material particles must correspondingly have a wavelength:

The de Broglie wavelength \[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} \]

The requirement that a standing wave must close on itself along a circular orbit, \(2\pi r = n\lambda\), gives exactly \(mvr = n\hbar\). Bohr's mysterious rule was in reality a consequence of matter being waves.

03 ·Schrödinger's equation

If matter is waves, there must be a wave equation. Schrödinger found it at Christmas 1925, and in its time-dependent form it reads:

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation \[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\psi \]

For stationary states it reduces to the time-independent form:

The time-independent form \[ \hat{H}\psi = E\psi \]

Written out for one particle in one spatial dimension, it reads:

One particle in one dimension \[ -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\,\frac{d^{2}\psi}{dx^{2}} + V(x)\,\psi = E\psi \]

Here the first term represents kinetic energy, while the second term, \(V(x)\), contains all the “physics” of the system as the potential energy.

04 ·The hydrogen atom – where the quantum numbers come from

The really big breakthrough for the Schrödinger equation was the solution for the hydrogen atom. Where Bohr had to postulate the quantisation, here the quantum numbers \((n, l, m_l)\) come out of the equation itself as an inescapable consequence of the requirement that the wave function must behave nicely everywhere. Schrödinger here explains why energy level \(n\) has \(n^{2}\) linearly independent states, which is the explanation of the structure of the periodic table and the basic rules of chemistry.

05 ·The cat and the new information paradigm

The wave function \(\psi\) is, however, not a wave in the usual sense. In 1935 Schrödinger formulated his famous thought experiment with a cat in a closed box to show the absurdity of the superposition of a living and a dead cat. He wanted to show that the interpretation could not be right.

We are, however, in the middle of a paradigm shift. In this new paradigm, information moves into the absolute centre of physics.

The cat is not physically both alive and dead; rather, it is our information about the cat's state that is in a superposition. Entropy and uncertainty are today understood as measures of missing information. The superposition does not end through a mysterious “collapse”, but through decoherence, in which information leaks out to the surroundings.

06 ·What do we actually call reality?

The question of what \(\psi\) “is” leads to a deeper question: what do we mean by “reality” at all when we demand that a theory must agree with it? We are 3D beings with limited senses who process signals into virtual mental models in our brains, which we then have the nerve to call reality.

Think of a rainbow. How can you describe it? You can describe it in prose or poetry, paint a picture of it or take a photograph of it, or use a spectrograph to analyse the wavelengths of light from its parts. Which of these is the truest description, the poetic or the scientific? They certainly complement each other, but none of them contains the whole truth about the rainbow – only the rainbow itself can do that. The point is that the scientific description, too, is a form of mapping, and that no map is the territory itself.

07 ·Imagination as method

Each of the decisive steps from 1911 to 1926 was a leap that could not be derived from what already existed: Bohr postulated, de Broglie asserted a symmetry, and Schrödinger guessed his way to an equation from an analogy.

“Imagination is more important than knowledge.” — Albert Einstein, literally true in the history of physics

If our teaching shows only the end product, we risk giving a picture of the subject that is pedagogically harmless, but historically false and humanly impoverished.

100 years on, Schrödinger's equation is still our workhorse, but the question of what the wave function “is” remains open – which is the finest tribute to the pioneers who showed us a universe so strange that we have not yet finished wondering.

Arianism and the Nicene faith

A conversation about the history of Arianism, the authority of the Church, and what is really at stake in the belief in the divinity of Christ — from the church conflict in Alexandria to the question of who can forgive sins, and on to the echoes in Islam, the Bahá'í Faith and other non-Nicene Christologies.

01 · Did the Arians have a »pope«?

Nicene Christianity continued an already existing institutional structure — the Bishop of Rome together with the other great patriarchates (Alexandria, Antioch, Constantinople, Jerusalem), all of which claimed apostolic succession and were in communion with one another. This structure was older than the Arian controversy and simply continued after Nicaea (325) condemned Arius.

Arianism, by contrast, spread through individual bishops and sees, not as a rival centralised church. Figures such as Eusebius of Nicomedia, and later Wulfila (who evangelised among the Goths), were influential, and for much of the 4th century Arian or semi-Arian bishops sat in important sees — among them Constantinople itself, with imperial backing under Constantius II. But no Arian counterpart to the papacy ever arose. When Germanic peoples (Goths, Vandals, Lombards) later adopted Arian Christianity, each kingdom had its own Arian clergy — again without any unifying head.

The asymmetry: Nicene Christianity continued Rome's already existing structure of primacy; Arianism remained a doctrinal movement spread across dioceses and kingdoms, without ever uniting in a single hierarchy.

02 · Are there Arians today?

Classical Arianism as an organised church died out around the 7th–8th century, when the last Arian Germanic kingdoms (especially the Lombards) converted to the Nicene faith. There is no unbroken institutional line to the present day.

The term is, however, used more loosely today of several non-Trinitarian movements:

  • Jehovah's Witnesses — the closest modern parallel; they regard Christ as a separate, created being, subordinate to the Father — although they themselves reject the label »Arian« as imprecise and claim no direct inheritance from Arius.
  • Unitarianism and related non-Trinitarian movements (certain Christadelphians, parts of the Christian wing of Unitarian Universalism) — reflect Arius' subordinationism independently.
  • Academically, »Arian« is sometimes used loosely of any denial of the full, coequal divinity of Christ — a theological category, not a claim of actual descent from the historical sect.

Conclusion: No self-declared Arian church survives, but the underlying theological position has resurfaced, independently, several times since.

03 · The three classic passages — and the Arian reading of them

TextNicene readingArian / non-Trinitarian reading
»Before Abraham was, I am«
(John 8:58)		 A deliberate echo of God's name (Exod 3:14); a claim to eternal, uncreated being. A claim to pre-existence and precedence, not identity with YHWH — Christ as the first and foremost of God's creatures.
»My Lord and my God«
(John 20:28)		 A direct confession of the full divinity of Christ, which Jesus lets stand uncorrected. An exclamation of astonishment or an expression of reverence addressed to God's supreme representative, or an address to the Father through Christ.
»The name above every name … every knee shall bow«
(Phil 2:9–11)		 A description of the incarnation of one who was already »in the form of God«. The text says that the name was given, and the status bestowed by God as a reward for obedience — the exaltation of a creature; note also that the bowing of the knee is »to the glory of God the Father«.

04 · »The Church chose the canon« — does the appeal to tradition settle the matter?

The objection: Scripture exists as canon only because the Church selected it; the oral apostolic tradition precedes the written one; therefore the texts must be read as the Church reads them — not interpreted outside this body.

To this: Arius was not an individual reading outside the Church — he was a presbyter in good standing in Alexandria, and after his condemnation he found support among other bishops, especially Eusebius of Nicomedia, who held one of the empire's most important sees. Arian and semi-Arian factions controlled large parts of the eastern episcopate for decades, among them Constantinople itself. The controversy was a struggle within the institutional Church about what the apostolic tradition actually taught — not »tradition versus private interpretation«.

The central books in this controversy (the Gospel of John, the Letter to the Philippians) were never disputed as canonical by either party — the Arian controversy was not a dispute about the canon, but an internal disagreement among people who agreed on the texts.

The deeper, unresolved question raised by the appeal to tradition: which body counts as »the Church« when several groups — all with bishops, councils and claims to apostolic succession — disagree among themselves? Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant traditions answer this differently, and this fault line runs beneath the whole debate.

05 · »Nicaea merely codified what the Fathers already taught«

The claim examined: Before Arius there was no alternative to belief in God's incarnation in Jesus; all the Church Fathers held this view; Nicaea merely stated an existing consensus.

Historical complications: Pre-Nicene Christology was not uniform. Modalism/Sabellianism (Father, Son and Spirit as the same person under different modes of appearance) and adoptionism (Jesus as a man, adopted or empowered by God) were both condemned as heresy before Arius — which in itself shows that the question was not yet settled. Logos subordinationism was widespread among writers normally regarded as broadly orthodox — Justin Martyr, Tertullian, and especially Origen, who spoke of the Son as begotten of the Father and in a sense subordinate in rank, albeit eternal.

Rowan Williams' influential study argues that Arius regarded himself as a defender of Origen's subordinationism against what he perceived as Bishop Alexander's innovations — that is, Arius himself believed that he was the one preserving the tradition, not the one breaking with it.

Nicaea's central term, homoousios (»of the same substance«), is found nowhere in Scripture, had earlier Gnostic associations, and had even been rejected at an earlier council in Antioch. Many bishops signed reluctantly or under imperial pressure from Constantine. Arian and semi-Arian positions then dominated large parts of the eastern church for more than another 50 years, requiring the Council of Constantinople (381) to settle the matter again.

Conclusion: Nicaea settled a real, long-standing ambiguity using new philosophical concepts — it did not merely restate an already existing, unanimous consensus.

06 · The Council of Jerusalem as a model (Acts 15)

The analogy: The Church has always engaged in self-reflection on its teaching and mission — as seen at the first apostolic council in Jerusalem, where Peter and Paul (despite real friction, cf. Gal 2) reached a binding decision: »It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us«.

Where the analogy holds: It shows that conciliar authority — the mechanism itself — is apostolic, not a later corruption. Nicaea stands in the same line.

»It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us« — Acts 15:28

Where it needs a qualification: Jerusalem settled a practical/disciplinary question (circumcision, dietary rules for Gentile Christians); Nicaea settled a question about Christ's ontological identity, which required philosophical concepts (ousia, hypostasis) not found in the apostolic preaching itself — a bigger leap, even if one recognises it as a legitimate development of doctrine rather than an invention. Jerusalem's decision was received relatively smoothly; Nicaea's was not — the decades of continued Arian dominance afterwards show a far more uneven reception, especially the dispute over the single iota that separates homoousios from homoiousios.

07 · With what authority could Arius, a presbyter, argue?

Formal position: Arius was in charge of the Baucalis church, one of the most important parishes in Alexandria, in a city whose ecclesiastical prestige ranked first after Rome. Alexandrian presbyters had real, considerable teaching authority.

Theological basis: Arius presented himself as a defender of the Alexandrian catechetical tradition rooted in Origen, and claimed that Bishop Alexander was the one innovating doctrine and blurring the distinction between Father and Son in a Sabellian direction.

Where his authority ended: A presbyter's authority was expressly subordinate to his bishop's. When Arius refused to recant, Alexander convened a synod of about 100 Egyptian and Libyan bishops (c. 320–321) and excommunicated him — at that point Arius had no formal position left in Alexandria.

What kept the controversy alive was not Arius' own authority (already ended by the decision of his bishop and a regional synod), but the fact that other bishops — especially Eusebius of Nicomedia, with connections at court — chose to make his cause their own, thereby turning a local disciplinary matter into an empire-wide crisis. In the Church's own terms this is the strongest structural argument against Arius: once bishop and synod had settled the matter, everything thereafter was schism, however many bishops later joined it.

08 · The core: Can a creature be the Good News?

The argument: To be a Christian is fundamentally to have a living relationship with God in the person of Jesus, not a text or a philosophy. Reason alone takes us only so far towards knowledge of God. Revealed truth is necessary, and this revelation is a person — who can be none other than God. Only God can forgive sins. God is not a being, however exalted, but Being itself — »I am who I am« — which is the metaphysical foundation of the Nicene formulation.

This is the deepest form of the Nicene argument:

  • Athanasius' argument (On the Incarnation): salvation is union with God, not information or moral guidance, and only God can achieve this union. Later sharpened to: »what is not assumed is not healed« (Gregory of Nazianzus) — if Christ is not fully human, humanity is not really touched; if he is not fully God, what touches humanity is not really God, and the union fails. That is why homoousios had existential, not merely philosophical, significance. Hence the doctrine of theosis: »God became man so that man might become god«.
  • The argument from the forgiveness of sins (Mark 2): when the scribes object »who can forgive sins but God alone?«, the text does not correct their premise — Jesus acts on the claim and gives a visible sign as proof of authority to do what only God can do. The logic of the narrative requires that this cannot be delegated to a creature, however exalted.
  • The metaphysical reading of »I am« (Exod 3:14, echoed in John 8:58): in a Thomist reading God is ipsum esse subsistens — Being itself, not one being among others. A creature's existence is always received and conditional; a creature could never truthfully say simply »I am«. If Jesus' use of the expression is deliberate, it is either a claim to be Being itself, or something exceedingly dangerous to say — in keeping with the crowd's reaction, as they immediately try to stone him.

How a consistent Arian would reply: by rejecting the dilemma — and maintaining that God, who cannot touch fallen matter directly, works through an exalted intermediary (the firstborn of creation, in unique closeness to the Father) to accomplish real revelation, real relationship and real grace, without this requiring identity of substance. Jehovah's Witnesses today would describe their relationship with Jesus in exactly these terms, while rejecting the inference that the role of intermediary requires ontological equality with the Father.

Where this leaves the debate: The fault line, at this deep level, is no longer the individual passages, but whether salvation requires the intermediary to be identical in substance with God, or whether a sufficiently exalted creature can mediate without this identity. This is a claim about the nature of God and of salvation — not merely a historical argument about who won a fourth-century church-political dispute. It is, in the end, as much a testimony as an argument: a claim to have met this Person — with Nicaea's formulation as the only account that does not ultimately reduce him to something less than what he did and does.

09 · Is Islam Arian?

The direct historical claim: John of Damascus, writing at the beginning of the 8th century under Muslim rule in Damascus, regarded Islam not as an independent religion but as a Christian heresy — more precisely a descendant of Arianism. In his work The Heresy of the Ishmaelites he wrote that Muhammad, »having come across the Old and New Testaments and, it seems, having conversed with an Arian monk, devised his own heresy«. This is connected with the later legend of Bahira, a monk (remembered in various sources as either Arian or Nestorian) who on a caravan journey to Syria supposedly recognised the young Muhammad's prophetic destiny. John explicitly identified Islam with Arianism, which denied the divinity of Christ and claimed that Jesus was created by God, neither coeternal nor of the same substance with him.

Why the comparison makes theological sense: The Qur'an's Christology does in fact run parallel to Arian territory. Jesus (Isa) is a prophet and Messiah, born of a virgin, but explicitly not divine and not God's »son« in any begetting sense — sura 112 states that God »neither begets nor is begotten«, and sura 4:171 warns: »do not say three« of the Trinity. The Qur'an maintains that Jesus is a prophet but cannot be God's son, because God cannot have helpers or offspring — structurally the Arian feature (denial of coequal, coeternal divinity), carried further.

Where the analogy breaks down: Arius still held a very high Christology — the Son as the pre-existent Logos, »begotten before all ages«, the means through which God created everything else, just not equal or coeternal with the Father. Islam rejects this whole scaffolding. Jesus is not an eternally begotten intermediary or the instrument of creation; he is one prophet among many (alongside Moses, Abraham, Muhammad), with no unique pre-existence. The Qur'an also denies that the crucifixion took place as the Christian narrative describes it (sura 4:157) — a claim without Arian parallels, since Arius never doubted the crucifixion, only Christ's ontological status.

Conclusion: calling Islam »Arian« is an old Christian polemical shorthand — partly an attempt to make Islam »just another heresy« rather than a genuinely new claim to revelation — which captures a real family resemblance (both reject the Trinity and the full, coequal divinity of Christ), but overstates the connection. Islam's Christology is its own thing, shaped by a broader rejection of divine sonship as such, rather than a subordinationist reading of it.

10 · The Bahá'í Faith and other non-Nicene Christologies

Bahá'í: not subordination, but a completely different category. The Bahá'í Faith regards Jesus (like Moses, Muhammad, Krishna, Buddha, Abraham and — for Bahá'ís — the Báb and Bahá'u'lláh) as a »Manifestation of God« (Mazhar-i-Ilahi): a being who perfectly reflects and reveals God's attributes to humanity, as a mirror reflects the sun without being the sun. God's essence is considered completely unknowable and transcendent — no Manifestation, not even Jesus, is God in essence or substance. At first sight this resembles Arianism (Jesus is not of the same substance as God), but the underlying logic is different: Arius worked within a Greek ontological framework of substance and begetting, whereas Bahá'í theology sets out a prophetology of recurring revelation — Jesus is not placed below God on a ladder of rank, but functions rather as one perfect mirror among several equally perfect mirrors throughout history, each suited to its age.

Other positions on the map:

  • Latter-day Saints (Mormons) are closer to the classical Arian structure than the Bahá'í Faith is: Jesus is truly divine, God's firstborn spirit child, separate in substance from the Father (not one metaphysical being, in contrast to Nicaea), pre-existent and the instrument of creation under the Father's direction — very close to Arius' own picture of a real, exalted, pre-existent Son who is not equal or coeternal in substance with the Father.
  • Christadelphians are adoptionists rather than true Arians — they reject Christ's personal pre-existence completely and hold that he came into being at his conception, a step even further from Nicaea than Arius went.
  • Swedenborgianism (the New Church) goes the opposite way — modalist/unitarian, with Jesus simply being God himself in human form, without any distinct divine persons at all: the Sabellian error, which the Nicene Fathers also fought, not the Arian one.
  • Ahmadiyya Islam inherits the mainstream Islamic Christology (Jesus as a prophet, not divine), but adds the distinctive claim that he survived the crucifixion and later died a natural death in Kashmir — a variation on the theme rather than a new position on divinity.

The recurring pattern: anyone who breaks with Nicaea must answer the same question — is Jesus a lower divine being on a ladder below God (Arius, the Mormons), a non-pre-existent human exalted by God (Christadelphians, adoptionism), one perfect mirror among several equal mirrors (the Bahá'í Faith), or simply God wearing a human face without any real distinction of persons (Swedenborgians, the Sabellians of the early church)? Nicaea's claim was that none of these four options preserves what the gospel needed to be true — hence the insistence on one Christ, true God and true man, in one person.

11 · The psychological analogy: God's self-knowledge and self-love as Trinity

The argument: God's self-understanding can be nothing other than God himself — anything else would be deficient. God's self-understanding is therefore the second person of the Trinity. Between these two persons there must be perfect love, and since this love is not deficient either, it too must be God. Three persons, one God.

This is in essence the Augustinian »psychological analogy« for the Trinity, later systematised by Thomas Aquinas.

Augustine's version. In De Trinitate (books 9–15) Augustine searches for vestigia Trinitatis — traces of the Trinity — in the human mind, precisely because man is created in the image of God. His basic figure is mens, notitia, amor (mind, self-knowledge, love), or in another formulation memoria, intellectus, voluntas (memory, understanding, will). The argument above follows exactly this logic: God's self-understanding cannot be less than God himself — any deficiency would mean that God did not fully know himself, which is unthinkable for the perfect being. This self-understanding is therefore the Word, the Logos, the Son — begotten, not made, of the Father's eternal self-knowledge (cf. John 1:1).

Thomas' sharpening. Aquinas turned this into a fixed conceptual framework: the Son proceeds per modum intellectus (by an act of knowing — the Father brings forth his own »mental word« about himself), while the Spirit proceeds per modum voluntatis (by an act of will — the mutual love between Father and Son). That is precisely the third movement of the argument: the love between the two persons cannot be deficient either, and a perfect love that really is God must itself be a person — the Spirit as vinculum amoris, the bond of love.

Two things worth noting about this line of thought:

  • It is specifically Western. The Eastern (Orthodox) tradition is sceptical about having the Spirit proceed from the love between Father and Son — this is the core of the filioque controversy that divided East and West in 1054. The Orthodox tradition maintains that the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, as sole source. This formulation stays close to the Latin, Augustinian-Thomist line.
  • Augustine himself was careful not to call it a proof. It is an analogy, a trace — not a derivation that reason alone could reach from scratch. This agrees with what was argued earlier in this conversation: reason can illuminate and deepen revealed truth, but does not produce it by itself. The Trinity is given in revelation; the psychological analogy helps us understand why it makes sense — not prove that it must be true.

In short: this is a fine and classic reconstruction of some of the deepest elements of Latin Trinitarian theology — God as a mind that knows itself perfectly and loves what it knows perfectly, where both the knowing and the love are so real that they cannot be less than God himself.

Summarised from a conversation about the history, ecclesiology and theology of the Arian controversy, its echoes in Islam, the Bahá'í Faith and other non-Nicene Christologies, and the Augustinian-Thomist psychological analogy for the Trinity.

Ground effect: why pelicans and aircraft float close to the surface

When a wing comes down to a height of less than about half its span above ground or water, its drag falls noticeably and it lifts a little better. Birds use this to save energy, pilots feel it when the aircraft “floats” just before landing, and Russian engineers built whole craft around it.

Why a wing has drag, even without friction

A wing lifts by pushing air downwards. The air under the wing has a higher pressure than the air above, and out at the wingtips it slips round from the underside to the upper side. That gives two rotating vortices, trailed behind the aircraft like two invisible tornadoes. They set the air behind the wing moving downwards, so the wing has to hold a slightly higher angle of attack to carry the same load. That costs an extra drag, the induced drag. It is largest at low speed, that is at take-off and landing, and for a bird in slow gliding flight.

The surface acts as a mirror

Close to the ground the air cannot flow freely downwards. Mathematically one can handle this by imagining a mirror wing below the surface with counter-rotating vortices (the method of images from electrostatics). The image vortices push upwards where the real ones push downwards, so the downwash behind the wing becomes weaker. The wing lifts a little more at the same angle, and the induced drag falls. At the same time the image vortices push the real vortices out sideways along the surface; you can see that in the animation.

The effect is felt below about half a wingspan and becomes strong below a tenth. At a tenth of the wingspan the induced drag in this model is about 28 % less than in free air; at a twentieth it is nearly halved.

Birds that fly on the air cushion

Pelicans glide in long lines just above the waves. Hainsworth (1988) filmed brown pelicans in Florida and found an average gliding height of 33 cm, a sixth of the 2.1 m wingspan. He estimated that they thereby saved about half the induced drag (other models give less, but the saving is clear). The skimmer drags its lower bill through the water and so always flies in ground effect. Shearwaters, puffins and sea ducks follow the contours of the waves, and over land it is seen less often, because grass and bushes are in the way.

Aircraft: floating on landing and false security on take-off

When an aircraft flares just above the runway, the drag falls and the aircraft tends to “float” some way further than expected. On take-off a heavily loaded aircraft can get airborne inside the ground effect but start to sink as it climbs out of it, because the drag suddenly grows. Pilots are therefore taught to hold the aircraft low and gain speed before climbing.

The ekranoplan: a ship that flies

In the 1960s the Soviet designer Rostislav Alekseyev built ekranoplans, large craft with short wings that fly a few metres above the water. The largest, the KM, known as the “Caspian Sea Monster”, was 92 m long, weighed up to 544 t and flew at about 430 km/h at a height of 4–14 m. It first flew in 1966 and crashed in 1980. The principle is efficient, but the craft are hard to control in waves and today are mostly used in smaller versions.

Another “ground effect”: racing cars

In Formula 1 the term means something else. There the floor of the car is shaped as a venturi channel, so the air under the car flows faster and gives a suction that pulls the car down onto the track. So it is a pressure drop under a body close to the ground, not less induced drag. Both come from the ground limiting how the air can flow.

About the animation

The wing is elliptic. The vortex cores sit at π/4 of the half-span out from the centre, and their path behind the wing is computed as a vortex pair in the cross-plane with image vortices below the surface (Lamb's classic solution). The particles follow the flow from the cores and their images.

The distance downstream is shown compressed, and the strength of the vortices is scaled so that the motion can be seen. The drag figures come from the formula in the diagram and apply only to the induced drag, not the friction drag.

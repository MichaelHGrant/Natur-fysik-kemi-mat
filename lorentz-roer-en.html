Sound in Ducts with Flow – Lorentz There and Back

When the gas in a duct flows, sound no longer obeys the ordinary wave equation. The usual Galilean transformation only gets you halfway: in the rest frame of the gas, the duct moves. A Lorentz transformation with the speed of sound in place of the speed of light removes the flow from the equation and keeps the walls at rest. You can then solve the familiar problem without flow and transform back. This page shows how – with three interactive 3D simulations of the duct, the expansion chamber and the lined duct.

The convected wave equation

The gas flows with constant velocity $V$ along the duct, and $M=V/c$ is the Mach number. Linearising the Euler and continuity equations about the flow, time derivatives become the material derivative $D/Dt=\partial_t+V\partial_x$, and the sound pressure satisfies

$$\frac{1}{c^2}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+V\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big)^{2}p-\nabla^2p=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial t^2}+\frac{2M}{c}\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x\,\partial t}-(1-M^2)\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2}-\nabla_{\!\perp}^2p=0 .$$

The mixed term $\partial^2p/\partial x\,\partial t$ makes the solutions asymmetric: sound with the flow behaves differently from sound against it. Throughout, the time factor is $e^{i\omega t}$, $k=\omega/c$ and $\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-M^2}$.

Galilei – and why not

The obvious step is to move into the rest frame of the gas, $x''=x-Vt$, $t''=t$. The equation does become the d'Alembert equation. But:

  • The wave equation is not Galilean invariant. That is exactly why the mixed term appears in the laboratory: the form of the equation depends on the frame.
  • The boundaries move. The ends of the duct, the steps of the chamber and the start and end of the lining are fixed in the laboratory – in the gas frame they slide backwards.
  • Frequency is not conserved. A downstream and an upstream wave with the same laboratory frequency have the frequencies $\omega/(1+M)$ and $\omega/(1-M)$ in the gas frame. One harmonic oscillation becomes two.

The Lorentz transformation

With the speed of sound $c$ in place of the speed of light, define

$$x'=\gamma x,\qquad t'=\frac{t}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma M}{c}\,x,\qquad y'=y,\quad z'=z .$$

The chain rule gives $\partial_t=\gamma^{-1}\partial_{t'}$ and $\partial_x=\gamma\,\partial_{x'}+(\gamma M/c)\,\partial_{t'}$, hence $\partial_t+V\partial_x=\gamma(\partial_{t'}+Mc\,\partial_{x'})$, because $1/\gamma+\gamma M^2=\gamma$. Inserting this, the mixed terms cancel, and since $\gamma^2(1-M^2)=1$ we are left with

$$\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2p}{\partial t'^2}-\frac{\partial^2p}{\partial x'^2}-\nabla_{\!\perp}^2p=0 .$$

The ordinary wave equation. The transformation only stretches $x$ by $\gamma$ and does not mix time into the spatial coordinate. The walls $y,z=$ const. stay at rest, and a duct segment $0\le x\le L$ becomes $0\le x'\le\gamma L$. The inverse is $x=x'/\gamma$, $t=\gamma t'-\gamma Mx'/c$.

Frequency is conserved

Writing $p=P(x)\,e^{i\omega t}$, we have $\omega t=\omega't'-KMx'$ with $\omega'=\gamma\omega$ and $K=\gamma k$. If we put

$$P(x,y,z)=\Phi(x',y,z)\,e^{\,iKMx'}=\Phi(\gamma x,y,z)\,e^{\,i\gamma^2kMx},$$

then $p=\Phi\,e^{i\omega't'}$ – one frequency – and $\Phi$ satisfies the ordinary Helmholtz equation $\partial_{x'}^2\Phi+\nabla_\perp^2\Phi+K^2\Phi=0$. The factor $e^{iKMx'}$ has a real wavenumber: it is a pure convective phase shift and never causes attenuation. That is exactly what the simulation shows: the asymmetry you see in the laboratory is this phase factor alone.

Hard ducts

In the Lorentz frame a mode is $\psi_{mn}(y,z)\,e^{\mp i\kappa x'}$ with $\kappa=\sqrt{K^2-k_{mn}^2}$, exactly as without flow. Back in the laboratory:

$$k_x^\pm=\gamma(\pm\kappa-MK)=\frac{-Mk\pm\sqrt{k^2-(1-M^2)k_{mn}^2}}{1-M^2}.$$
  • Plane wave: $k_x^+=k/(1+M)$ and $k_x^-=-k/(1-M)$ – the phase speeds are $c+V$ and $c-V$.
  • Cut-on frequency: the mode propagates when $K>k_{mn}$, i.e. $f>f_{mn}\sqrt{1-M^2}$. The flow lowers all cut-on frequencies, equally in both directions.
  • Particle velocity: $u_\pm=\pm p_\pm/\rho_0c$ – the characteristic impedance is unchanged. The power is $W_\pm=S|p_\pm|^2(1\pm M)^2/2\rho_0c$ (Morfey 1971).

The transfer matrix – there and back

A duct segment of length $L$ is, in the Lorentz frame, an ordinary segment of length $\gamma L$ at wavenumber $\gamma k$. Back in the laboratory it becomes

$$\begin{pmatrix}p\\ q\end{pmatrix}_{\!0}=e^{-i\gamma^2kML}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\gamma^2kL & iY\sin\gamma^2kL\\ iY^{-1}\sin\gamma^2kL & \cos\gamma^2kL\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\ q\end{pmatrix}_{\!L},\qquad Y=\frac{\rho_0c}{S}.$$

The no-flow matrix – just with $kL\to kL/(1-M^2)$ and a phase factor of modulus 1. Nothing new has to be solved.

The expansion chamber

Inlet and outlet ducts have area $S_1$ and Mach number $M_1$; the chamber has area $mS_1$, length $L$ and Mach number $M_k=M_1/m$. With continuity of pressure and volume velocity at the steps, the phase factor drops out, and the power factor $(1+M_1)^2$ is the same in inlet and outlet. What remains is the classical formula:

$$\mathrm{TL}=10\log_{10}\Big[1+\tfrac14\Big(m-\frac1m\Big)^{2}\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{kL}{1-M_k^2}\Big)\Big].$$

The maximum is unchanged, but the peaks move down to $f_n=(2n-1)\,c\,(1-M_k^2)/4L$.

The lined duct

For a locally reacting lining with impedance $\zeta=Z/\rho_0c$ and flow right up to the wall, continuity of particle displacement is required (Ingard 1959, Myers 1980). For a duct of height $2h$, lined on both sides, and a mode $\cos(qy)$, this gives

$$q\tan(qh)=\frac{ik}{\zeta}\Big(1-\frac{Mk_x}{k}\Big)^{2},\qquad (k-Mk_x)^2=k_x^2+q^2 .$$

In the Lorentz frame $q$ is unchanged, and $k-Mk_x^\pm=\gamma(K\mp M\kappa)$. The boundary condition therefore becomes exactly that of a lined duct without flow, at the frequency $\gamma\omega$ and with an effective impedance:

$$q\tan(qh)=\frac{iK}{\zeta'_\pm},\qquad \zeta'_\pm=\frac{\zeta}{\gamma^3\,(1\mp M\kappa/K)^2}.$$

Back in the laboratory the attenuation is $\alpha_\pm=\gamma\,|\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa|$ – the phase factor does not contribute. For a weak lining ($|1/\zeta|\ll kh$) this gives a closed result:

$$\alpha_\pm=\frac{\alpha_0}{(1\pm M)^2},\qquad \frac{\alpha_-}{\alpha_+}=\Big(\frac{1+M}{1-M}\Big)^{2}.$$

Compare with $\alpha_0/(1\pm M)$ on Relativistic acoustics, where the damping is a property of the gas. With a lining, the boundary condition gives an extra factor $1/(1\pm M)$: the displacement of the wall is seen Doppler-shifted by the flowing gas. At $M=0.3$ the ratio of upstream to downstream attenuation is $3.45$, against $1.86$ in the gas model.

The peak moves because, in the Lorentz frame, the lining is seen at the frequency $\gamma\omega$ and with an impedance scaled by $1/\gamma^3(1\mp M)^2$: upstream it becomes effectively “softer”, and the peak lies lower.

The recipe

  1. The problem in the laboratory: frequency $\omega$, lengths $L$, cross-section, Mach number $M$, lining impedance $\zeta$.
  2. There: $\omega'=\gamma\omega$, $L'=\gamma L$, transverse dimensions unchanged, $\zeta\to\zeta'_\pm$.
  3. Solve the usual problem without flow.
  4. Back: multiply by $e^{iKMx'}$: $k_x^\pm=\gamma(\pm\kappa-MK)$, $kL\to kL/(1-M^2)$, $\alpha=\gamma|\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa|$.
  5. Power: $W_\pm\propto(1\pm M)^2|p_\pm|^2$ if inlet and outlet have different Mach numbers.

Caveats and sources

  • The junction conditions in the expansion chamber are an approximation; with flow, the flow separates at the step, and Munjal (2014) adds a pressure loss. This matters most for $M_1\gtrsim0.2$.
  • Myers' boundary condition assumes plug flow right up to the wall. A real boundary layer weakens the effect, so Myers gives an upper bound on the asymmetry.
  • All numbers have been checked: the transfer matrix and the closed formula agree to $10^{-9}$ dB, and the lined-duct solutions have been inserted in the laboratory-frame equations with a residual below $10^{-6}$.

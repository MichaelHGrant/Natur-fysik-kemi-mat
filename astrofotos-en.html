M106 · NGC 4258

M106 with the active heart

M106 is a large spiral galaxy in Canes Venatici with a special secret: in its core sits a supermassive black hole of about 40 million solar masses (MBH ≈ 4 · 107 M☉), surrounded by a disc of water masers. It is these masers that have made M106 one of the most important objects for measuring the expansion of the universe – the so-called Hubble constant, H0 ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc.

M64 · NGC 4826

The Black Eye Galaxy

M64 in Coma Berenices — one of the most characteristic objects of the spring sky. The galaxy got its nickname from the striking dark dust lane that lies in front of the bright core and absorbs the light behind it, so that it looks like a "black eye". M64 hides an even stranger secret: its inner gas rotates one way, while the outer gas rotates the opposite way — a clear fingerprint of an encounter with a smaller satellite galaxy about a billion years ago.

A close crop of M64, in which the famous dark dust lane stands out sharply as a wedge in front of the bright core — it is precisely this structure that has given the galaxy its name. Albireo · β Cygni

The jewel of Cygnus

Albireo marks the head of the Swan and is regarded as one of the most beautiful double stars in the sky. In this short 20-second image the two components merge into a single bright point – but in reality Albireo consists of a golden K giant and a hot blue companion, separated by about 35 arcseconds. New measurements from the Gaia satellite suggest that the two stars are in fact not bound by gravity – they merely lie in the same direction seen from the Earth, but at different distances.

The giant of the Solar System

Partial solar eclipse

When the Moon took a bite out of the Sun

On 12 August 2026 the Moon pushed in front of the Sun, as seen from Denmark. Here the eclipsed Sun stands low and deep red through the thin evening clouds, shortly before sunset — with a large dark bite cut out of the disc by the Moon's silhouette. From Denmark up to 86 % of the Sun was covered at maximum; totality itself lay far to the west and south, over Iceland and northern Spain. A foretaste of the total eclipse I am travelling to Spain to experience on 2 August 2027.

The ringed one

Even as a small disc far out in the telescope, Saturn's ring stands free of the globe — the most beautiful sight in the Solar System. The rings are not solid, but billions of pieces of ice, from grains of sand to houses, orbiting the planet in a layer that in most places is only from ten metres to a kilometre thick. Saturn itself is a gas giant so light that it would float, if one could find an ocean big enough.

The autumn Moon

The evening just after the autumn equinox, a little more than a week after new Moon. The terminator now cuts through the western part of the disc, where the highland craters stand out in the low-angled light, while the great dark seas — Mare Imbrium, Serenitatis and Tranquillitatis — lie fully lit. The maria are frozen lava plains from eruptions more than three billion years ago; the bright regions are the older, heavily cratered crust.

Full Moon

The harvest Moon in full light

The full Moon nearest the autumn equinox is called the harvest Moon. With the Sun directly behind us, the shadows almost vanish and the ray systems come out instead: the bright streaks of ejected material around young craters such as Tycho at the bottom and Copernicus left of centre. Tycho's rays stretch across much of the visible hemisphere — the trace of an impact only about 100 million years ago.

The morning Moon

Taken at half past four in the morning, four days after full Moon. Now it is the opposite side of the disc that is going into darkness: the terminator lies to the east, and the craters along it once again cast long shadows. The waning Moon rises late and is best seen in the early morning hours — which is why it is photographed far less often than the waxing Moon.

M27 · NGC 6853

The Dumbbell Nebula

The first planetary nebula ever discovered — Charles Messier found it in 1764. The name is misleading: the nebula has nothing to do with planets, but consists of the outer layers of a dying star blown out into space. The hot white dwarf at the centre makes the gas glow, hydrogen red and oxygen blue-green. It lies in Vulpecula in the middle of the Milky Way, which is why the field is packed with stars.

The apple core up close

The full sensor field shows the nebula's characteristic shape, which some call a dumbbell and others an apple core: two bright lobes with a narrower waist. The red rim is ionised hydrogen, the blue-green interior doubly ionised oxygen. The gas is expanding at about 30 km/s, and the nebula is roughly 10,000 years old — a moment in the life of a star.

NGC 7293 · The Helix

The eye of God

One of the nearest planetary nebulae and almost half the size of the full Moon in the sky — but so faint and so low in the south that it is a challenge from Denmark. From 56° north it never rises more than about 13° above the horizon, so its light has to pass through a thick layer of atmosphere. Even so, the red ring appeared after six minutes: a shell of gas around a white dwarf that we see almost face-on.

The Whirlpool in autumn

The Whirlpool Galaxy again — in May it stood high in the sky, now in October it was low in the north-west. With 27 minutes of exposure the two spiral arms and the small companion NGC 5195 come out clearly. It was Lord Rosse who in 1845 first saw the spiral structure here, and so discovered that some 'nebulae' are spirals.

The Crab Nebula

The remains of a star that exploded as a supernova in the year 1054. Chinese astronomers recorded a 'guest star' visible in broad daylight for more than three weeks. Today we see an expanding cloud of gas — and at its centre a pulsar, a neutron star the size of a city spinning about 30 times a second.

The Moon's phases in a row

Five of the autumn's Moon images arranged by the Moon's age rather than by the date they were taken: first quarter (7 days), waxing gibbous (10 days), full Moon (15 days), waning gibbous (18 days) and the last crescent (26 days). Together they show a whole lunar cycle at a glance: the light grows from the right, fills the disc and withdraws towards the left. The boundary between light and dark — the terminator — is where the Sun is rising or setting on the Moon, and where the craters cast their longest shadows.

Machines that
harvest motion.

A turbine is one of mankind's most elegant inventions: a rotating machine that extracts energy from a flowing fluid — air, water or hot gas — and delivers it as rotation. The principle is the same everywhere, but the physics behind it, the geometry and the efficiency vary drastically. Here is how they work — illustrated, animated and spiced with Danish examples.

01 — The common principle

A flow meets a blade.

Every single turbine in the world is based on the same idea: a fluid passes a set of blades shaped so that the fluid either pushes the blades (impulse) or lifts them like aircraft wings (reaction). The result is rotation — and rotation is the easiest form of energy to convert further into electricity.

Energy in motion

The basic energy in a flowing fluid is kinetic: ½mv². When the mass is a constant flow — air, water or combustion gas — the power becomes what we harvest: power = ½ × mass flow × v². The mass flow depends on the density of the fluid and the area the turbine "captures" — that is why wind turbines are enormous and water turbines small: water is about 800 times denser than air.

Power from a flowing fluid
P = ½ · ρ · A · v³ · η
ρ — density of the fluid (kg/m³)
A — flow area (m²)
v — flow speed (m/s)
η — efficiency of the turbine (0–1)

Impulse vs. reaction

There are two basic families. An impulse turbine gets all its energy from the speed of the fluid: a high-pressure jet hits a cup and delivers its momentum (Pelton, de Laval's steam turbine). A reaction turbine uses aerodynamic lift: the blade is fully immersed in the fluid and harvests lift along its whole body, and part of the pressure drop occurs in the runner itself (Kaplan, Francis, Parsons' steam turbine; the blades of a wind turbine work in the same way with lift).

The difference sounds academic, but is practical: impulse turbines are simple and robust, but require high speed in the fluid. Reaction turbines can harvest from slow, large flows — that is why modern wind turbines have become giants.

02 — Wind turbines

The blade is an aerofoil.

A modern wind turbine is not a mill. It is an inverted aircraft engine: three twisted aerofoils that turn because the air passing them creates lift. The physics: why v³?

The kinetic energy in the wind is ½mv². But the mass flow through the rotor area is also proportional to v — the faster the wind blows, the more air passes per second. Energy per second (power) therefore becomes proportional to v² × v = v³. It is the most important rule of wind power: doubling the wind speed gives eight times as much power. That is why good wind sites are worth billions — and why modern wind turbines are placed in the North Sea, where the wind is strong and steady.

Wind power with the Betz limit
P = ½ · ρ · π·r² · v³ · Cp
ρ ≈ 1.225 kg/m³ (air at sea level) · r — rotor radius · v — wind speed
Cp — power coefficient, fundamentally limited by Betz' law to 16/27 ≈ 59.3 %
Modern turbines: Cp ≈ 45–50 % at the optimal operating point

Betz' law — the inescapable limit

In 1919 Albert Betz proved that no wind turbine can harvest more than 16/27 ≈ 59.3 % of the energy of the wind. The logic is simple: if the turbine took all the energy out of the wind, the air would stop behind the rotor — and then there would be no room for new air to flow in. For the wind to keep flowing through, some of its energy must remain afterwards. The optimal balance — where the product of flow and braking is greatest — gives exactly 16/27. It is physics' answer to "how much can you eat and still invite more people to the table."

Horns Rev 1, 2 and 3

North Sea · 160 + 209 + 407 MW

Horns Rev 1 was the world's first large offshore wind farm (2002). Three generations off Esbjerg, together covering over 100 km² of sea. The wind here is among the most constant in Europe.

Roskilde · since the 1970s

Risø's test station for wind turbines (from 1978) helped make the three-bladed, stall-regulated turbine — the so-called Danish concept — the standard. Risø's wind atlas method is still the industry standard worldwide.

03 — Water turbines

Gravity does the hard work.

A water turbine uses potential energy: water that has been lifted up by rain and evaporation, and which gains speed through a fall. Water is about 800 times denser than air, so even small amounts of water can be harvested with high power. Three main types dominate — choose one below:

Pelton, Francis, Kaplan — one formula, three geometries

All three turbines follow the same formula: the power is the product of head, volume of water, density, gravity and efficiency. But the three geometries each cover their own operating domain. Pelton is an impulse turbine — best when the head is extreme (hundreds or thousands of metres) and the volume of water moderate: a concentrated high-speed jet hits bowl-shaped buckets. Francis is a mixed radial-axial reaction turbine that dominates the middle range (40–600 m head, large plants). Kaplan resembles a ship's propeller and is built for low head and large volumes of water — as most Danish rivers would give.

Power of the water turbine
P = η · ρ · g · Q · H
ρ = 1000 kg/m³ (water) · g = 9.81 m/s² · Q — volume flow (m³/s) · H — net head (m)
η — efficiency of the turbine, typically 85–95 % (Francis can reach 96 % at the optimal point)

Denmark's largest hydroelectric power station. Three Francis turbines, 10 metres of head, originally built 1918–21. Tangeværket still supplies the grid — and is the centre of a long debate about removing the dam and restoring the natural course of the Gudenå river.

Case · Classic

Karlsgårde hydroelectric power station

Varde Å · 1921 · 4 × Francis

One of the other original plants from the interwar period. Still in operation with its original Francis turbines, and a living industrial museum.

Case · International

Norway as a water battery

The Skagerrak cables · about 1,700 MW

Denmark has no pumped-storage power stations. Instead we use Norway's reservoirs as a battery: when it is very windy here, the Norwegians buy our electricity and save their water; when it is calm, their water turbines send power the other way.

04 — Gas turbines

Combustion on the shaft.

A gas turbine is in principle a jet engine placed stationary. Air is drawn in, compressed by many small blades, mixed with fuel, ignited — and the hot high-pressure gas expands through the turbine, which both drives the compressor and delivers surplus work to the generator. The whole cycle is known as the Brayton cycle.

The Brayton cycle — four steps on the shaft

The cycle consists of four processes that run continuously: (1) Intake and compression — air is drawn in and compressed to 15–30 times atmospheric pressure, which naturally heats it. (2) Combustion at constant pressure — natural gas or biofuel is injected and ignited, so that the temperature rises to 1400–1600 °C. (3) Expansion through the turbine — the hot high-pressure gas expands over several stages with large blades and delivers work to the shaft. (4) Exhaust — the flue gas leaves the machine at around 600 °C, still full of usable heat.

Ideal Brayton efficiency
η = 1 − (1 / r(γ−1)/γ)
r — pressure ratio (typically 15–30 in industrial use)
γ — adiabatic exponent for air ≈ 1.4
At r = 15 the formula gives η ≈ 53 % theoretically — real losses give 35–40 % in a simple cycle

Combined cycle — the heat gets a second life

The exhaust at 600 °C is a thermodynamic scandal if it is thrown away. A combined cycle power plant (CCGT) captures this heat in a boiler, boils water and drives a classic steam turbine afterwards. The result: up to 62 % overall efficiency — the highest in industrial practice. It is the Brayton cycle + the Rankine cycle in series.

Danish examples

Case · Central power station

Avedøreværket

Hvidovre · unit 2 · Ørsted

One of the world's most efficient combined heat and power stations. Unit 2 runs on multiple fuels (natural gas, oil, straw, wood pellets) and achieves up to 49 % electrical efficiency, or 94 % when district heating is included — combined heat and power.

Case · Reserve power

Gas turbines as reserve

Fast-start reserve power

Wind power's weakness is calm days. Gas turbines can reach full power in 10–15 minutes and are therefore used as reserve power when the wind fails — far faster than a coal- or biomass-fired steam power station, which has to be heated up for hours.

Case · Conversion

Studstrupværket

Aarhus Bay · originally coal, now wood pellets

An example of the Danish transition: built as a coal-fired power station in 1968–84, now converted to wood pellets and biomass. Although the fuel changes, the steam turbine and the whole infrastructure are the same — modular thermodynamics.

Case · Future

Hydrogen and Power-to-X

Esbjerg · planned 1 GW

Danish wind power is converted into green hydrogen via electrolysis, which can then be burned in adapted gas turbines — or form the basis for ammonia and e-fuels. The future of the gas turbine will probably be green.

Hydropower almost always comes top — the fluid is dense, the loss in the blades is minimal, and there is no thermodynamic Carnot limit in the way. Combined-cycle gas comes second because it combines two cycles. Wind peaks at the Betz ceiling of 59.3 %, but loses only a little in the machine itself — and besides, the fuel is free and unlimited, so the lower efficiency is irrelevant economically and for the climate.

06 — Turbines in Denmark

Where does it flow from?

Denmark is one of the most turbine-dependent countries in the world — almost all electricity production takes place via some kind of rotating machine, and the distribution tells a story of green transition and geography:

55%
Wind power · 2024

More than half of Danish electricity production. Just over 6,000 wind turbines, of which around 600 are offshore. The world's highest share of wind.

~22%
Thermal turbines

Steam and gas turbines on biomass, natural gas and waste. Mostly combined heat and power — electricity and district heating in combination.

>10%
Solar cells (no turbine!)

The only major source of electricity in Denmark without a rotating part. Growing strongly — direct photovoltaic conversion.

<0.1%
Hydropower

17 MW total. Tangeværket and Karlsgårde keep the tradition alive, but the topography sets a hard limit.

The green paradox: the wind is uneven

With 55 % wind in the grid, the electricity price becomes very volatile — it falls towards zero when it is very windy and rises when it is calm. This has made Denmark a pioneer in sector coupling: when there is too much wind power, the surplus is converted into district heating via large electric heat pumps, into hydrogen via electrolysis, or exported via submarine cables to Norway (hydropower as a battery), Germany and the Netherlands.

Why turbines still win

Even though solar cells are growing explosively, rotating turbines will account for most of Danish electricity production for many decades to come. There is also a physical reason to hold on to rotating machines: the large synchronous generators in thermal and hydroelectric power stations have rotational inertia — large rotating masses that stabilise the grid frequency of 50 Hz. Solar cells have no rotating mass, and modern wind turbines are connected to the grid through power electronics, so they do not contribute directly. The green future will most likely be a mix: wind turbines, gas turbines on hydrogen as reserve power, synchronous condensers (rotating machines without a turbine that only provide inertia) and inverters that imitate inertia electronically.

El Niño and La Niña: when the Pacific tips

Every few years the eastern Pacific off Peru becomes a couple of degrees warmer than normal. The trade winds weaken, the rain moves east, and the weather changes over half the globe. This is El Niño. Afterwards the opposite, La Niña, often follows. Here the oscillation is computed live with the recharge oscillator, and the section along the equator shows the ocean, the thermocline and the wind.

The boy child

Long ago the fishermen of Peru and Ecuador noticed that in some years the sea became warm around Christmas and the anchovies disappeared. They called the current El Niño, the boy child, after the Christ child. Normally the trade winds blow west along the equator and push the warm surface water over towards Indonesia. Off South America cold, nutrient-rich water rises from the depths, the upwelling, and it gives some of the richest fishing grounds in the world.

The thermocline and the Walker circulation

Under the warm surface layer the temperature changes abruptly to cold deep water. The transition is called the thermocline. Because the trade winds pile the warm water up in the west, the thermocline lies about 150 m down at Indonesia and only 30–50 m down off Peru. The sea surface is even about half a metre higher in the west than in the east.

Over the warm water in the west the air rises and gives heavy rain. It flows east at 10–15 km height, sinks over the cold water off South America and returns as the trade winds. This is the Walker circulation, named after Gilbert Walker, who in the 1920s found that the air pressure over the Indian Ocean and over the eastern Pacific swings in opposite phase: the Southern Oscillation. Today it is measured as the pressure difference between Tahiti and Darwin.

The Bjerknes feedback

In 1969 the Norwegian-American meteorologist Jacob Bjerknes showed that the ocean and the atmosphere reinforce each other. If the eastern Pacific becomes a little warmer, the temperature difference along the equator shrinks, and the trade winds weaken. Then less warm water is pushed west, the thermocline in the east deepens, the upwelling brings up warmer water, and the east becomes warmer still. The same coupling works the other way and gives La Niña. The Bjerknes coupling slider sets how strong this feedback is.

The recharge oscillator

So what stops El Niño? During El Niño warm water flows away from the equator towards the poles, and the warm layer across the whole equatorial Pacific gets thinner: it discharges. After a year or two there is not enough warm water to keep El Niño going, and the cold deep water breaks through: La Niña. During La Niña the trade winds pile up warm water in the west again, and the warm layer recharges. Fei-Fei Jin described it in 1997 with two equations:

dT/dt = R·T + γ·h + noise
dh/dt = −r·h − α·T

T is the sea temperature anomaly in the east, h is the thermocline depth in the west, R = μ − λ is the Bjerknes coupling minus the damping, and γ, α and r are constants. Without damping it is a harmonic oscillation with the period 2π/√(γα), about four years. In the phase plane the oscillation runs round clockwise: recharged, El Niño, discharged, La Niña. The weather gives small pushes all the time, which makes the oscillation irregular, with 2–7 years between events.

Kelvin waves

A westerly wind burst, a few days of strong westerly wind in the western Pacific, sends a Kelvin wave east along the equator. It pushes the thermocline down and travels at about 2.5 m/s, crossing the Pacific in two to three months. When it reaches South America, the upwelling becomes warmer. Many El Niño events have been set off in this way.

Weather around the globe

When the rain moves east, the whole wave pattern of the atmosphere shifts. During El Niño it typically becomes dry in Indonesia, Australia and southern Africa, and the Indian monsoon is often weaker. Heavy rain and floods come to Peru and Ecuador and, in winter, to the southern United States. Fewer hurricanes form in the Atlantic, because the wind shear increases, and more in the central Pacific. See it on the page on hurricanes and typhoons. La Niña gives largely the opposite. The Earth's mean temperature rises by 0.1–0.2 °C in the year after a strong El Niño. In Europe and Denmark the connection is weak.

Measurement

The sea temperature along the equator is measured by satellites and by a chain of buoys, the TAO/TRITON array, built after the El Niño of 1982–83, which nobody had seen coming. NOAA's ONI index is the three-month mean of the anomaly in the Niño 3.4 region.

What the model computes

The oscillation is the recharge oscillator with random wind bursts, a small nonlinear term that makes El Niño stronger than La Niña, and a growth that is largest in autumn, so that events peak around Christmas. The map and the section use the same simple sea temperature climatology as the page on hurricanes, with El Niño's pattern added in proportion to T. The tilt of the thermocline, the trade winds and the Walker circulation follow the temperature difference along the equator.

Limitations

The real oscillation has many more degrees of freedom: Rossby waves reflected at Asia, patterns that peak in the central Pacific, and interplay with the Indian Ocean. The model shows the mechanism, not a forecast.

From diffraction to image

A lens does not just make an image. It first spreads the light out into a diffraction pattern in its focal plane – and then gathers it again. Here you can step in between the two stages and cut into the light.

Six experiments to start with

Each experiment sets up the optical bench for you and tells you what to look for.

1The idea: the image is built in two stages

In 1873 Ernst Abbe sat at Carl Zeiss in Jena trying to understand why some microscope objectives showed fine details and others did not, even though they were equally well ground. The answer became a completely new way of looking at image formation.

When parallel light hits an object – a grating, a cell, a piece of text – it is scattered in many directions. Each direction corresponds to a particular level of detail in the object: coarse features send the light a little to the side, fine details send it far out. A lens gathers all light travelling in the same direction into one point in its focal plane. That is why the focal plane contains a map of the object's directions – this is the Fraunhofer diffraction pattern, which would otherwise only appear infinitely far away.

The light does not stop there. Each point of light in the focal plane sends a new wave onwards, and where the waves meet again, they interfere and form the image. The image is thus the diffraction pattern of the diffraction pattern. The optical bench above shows this as a so-called 4f setup: object, lens, focal plane, lens and image, with the distance f between each element.

The important consequence: anything that does not get through the focal plane can never appear in the image. The edge of a lens, an iris or a piece of tape in the focal plane directly determines which details the image includes.

2The mathematics: the lens is a Fourier transformer

Let the transmission of the object be \(t(x,y)\) – a complex function that tells how much of the light's amplitude gets through, and how much the phase is shifted. With the object in the front focal plane of the lens, the field in the back focal plane becomes

\[ U(x_F,y_F) \;\propto\; \iint t(x,y)\, e^{-i2\pi\,(u x + v y)}\,dx\,dy \;=\; T(u,v), \qquad u=\frac{x_F}{\lambda f},\quad v=\frac{y_F}{\lambda f}. \]

This is exactly the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the object. The quantities \(u\) and \(v\) are spatial frequencies – number of lines per millimetre – and a point in the focal plane at the distance \(x_F\) from the axis corresponds to the frequency \(u = x_F/(\lambda f)\).

A grating with the period \(p\) has only the frequencies \(0, \pm 1/p, \pm 2/p, \dots\). In the focal plane this gives spots of light at the distances

\[ x_m = m\,\frac{\lambda f}{p}, \qquad m = 0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots \]

– the familiar diffraction orders, now simply gathered in a plane instead of on a distant wall. The second lens performs another Fourier transform. Two transforms in succession give the function back, but mirrored: \(\mathcal{F}\{\mathcal{F}\{t\}\}(x,y) = t(-x,-y)\). That is why the image is upside down. If the lenses have different focal lengths \(f_1\) and \(f_2\), the image is magnified by \(f_2/f_1\).

If we insert a filter \(H(x_F,y_F)\) in the focal plane, the image field becomes the inverse transform of \(T\cdot H\). That is what this page calculates every time you move a slider.

3The resolution limit

Abbe's most important result follows directly: for a grating to be seen as stripes at all, both the 0th order and at least one of the 1st orders must reach the image. With only the 0th order there is no interference, and the image is uniformly bright. If the aperture in the focal plane has radius \(R\), then \(\lambda f/p \le R\), so the smallest period reproduced is

\[ p_{\min} = \frac{\lambda f}{R} = \frac{\lambda}{\mathrm{NA}}, \qquad \mathrm{NA} = n\sin\alpha \approx \frac{R}{f}. \]

If the object is illuminated obliquely, so that the 0th order lies at the edge of the aperture, the opposite 1st order can come in from the other edge, and the limit is halved to the famous

\[ d = \frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}}. \]

With green light and an oil-immersion objective (NA ≈ 1.4) it is about 0.2 µm. It is not a question of better grinding – it is wave physics. The formula is on Abbe's memorial in Jena. Note also the \(\lambda\) in the numerator: a shorter wavelength gives finer details. That is why electron microscopes (see the optics page) can see atoms.

4The Airy disc: the image of a point

The connection also works the other way. If the object is a single luminous point – a star – its Fourier transform is completely uniform, and it is the aperture of the lens that determines what gets through. The image of the point therefore becomes the Fourier transform of the lens aperture itself. For a circular aperture with diameter \(D\) this is the Airy pattern: a bright disc surrounded by faint rings, with the first dark ring at

\[ \sin\theta = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}. \]

This is Rayleigh's criterion for when two stars can be separated. In the experiment The Airy disc above it is the other way round: the object is a small round hole, and the pattern in the focal plane is its Fourier transform. The mathematics is the same.

The same idea explains the diffraction spikes in star images: the holder of the secondary mirror in a reflecting telescope consists of thin rods, and a thin rod has a Fourier transform that is a long line perpendicular to the rod. Four rods give four spikes. And the Bahtinov mask works because its three gratings give three lines in the focal plane, which only stand symmetrically when the focus is exact.

5The Abbe–Porter experiment

Abbe demonstrated his theory with the microscope, but the classic experiment was made clear by Albert Porter in 1906. A fine wire mesh is illuminated with parallel light. In the focal plane you see a square grid of spots of light: the vertical wires give a horizontal row of spots, the horizontal wires a vertical row.

If you place a horizontal slit in the focal plane, only the horizontal row gets through. In the image the horizontal wires have disappeared – only the vertical ones remain. Turn the slit 90°, and it is the other way round. Let only a diagonal row pass, and the image shows diagonal wires that do not exist in the mesh at all! Try it with Paint your own with black tape: cover everything except a diagonal row of spots.

The experiment is the clearest proof that the image really is assembled from the diffraction orders. It is the basis of all optical filtering – from removing halftone dots in old newspaper photos to pattern recognition with light.

6Phase contrast: Zernike's Nobel Prize

A living cell in water is almost completely transparent. It absorbs nothing, but delays the light slightly, because its refractive index is higher. The transmission is \(t = e^{i\varphi(x,y)}\), and since \(|t|^2 = 1\), the cell is invisible in an ordinary microscope. Choose Transparent cells without a filter: the image is uniform.

For a weak phase \(t \approx 1 + i\varphi\). The one is the undiffracted light, which is gathered in the central spot in the focal plane. The term \(i\varphi\) is the diffracted light, and the factor \(i\) means that it is 90° out of step with the central light. That is why they do not interfere in the intensity. In the 1930s Frits Zernike realised that a small plate in the focal plane that only delays the central spot by a quarter of a wavelength multiplies the one by \(i\):

\[ i + i\varphi = i\,(1+\varphi) \quad\Rightarrow\quad I \approx 1 + 2\varphi. \]

Now the phase has become brightness, and the cell stands out. Zernike received the Nobel Prize in Physics for this in 1953. If you instead block the central spot completely (dark field), \(I \approx \varphi^2\): the cells glow faintly on a black background. You can try both on the optical bench.

7Try it yourself – without a laboratory

Your own eye is a pocket-sized 4f setup: the lens of the eye focuses at infinity, and the retina sits in the focal plane. Look at a distant street lamp through a piece of dense curtain, an umbrella or a fine door screen. The lamp is a point, so what you see is the Fourier transform of the fabric: a square grid of spots of light. Turn the fabric, and the pattern turns with it. Stretch it, and the spots move closer together – frequency and period are inversely proportional.

With a laser pointer, a magnifying glass and a piece of tulle you can do the Abbe–Porter experiment: put the tulle in the beam, find the focal plane behind the lens with a piece of paper, and lay a slit made of two pieces of tape over the spots. With an extra lens the image of the tulle can be caught on a wall.

Protect your eyes: Never look into a laser, not even into a reflected beam. Use a weak pointer (class 2, below 1 mW) and let the spots land on paper or a wall.

At the telescope you can see the Airy disc directly: use high magnification on a bright star on a calm night. Put a cardboard mask with a smaller hole in front of the telescope, and the rings get bigger – smaller aperture, wider Fourier transform.

8Exercises

1. The spots from a grating

A grating with a period of 0.25 mm is illuminated with a green laser (532 nm). The lens has a focal length of 200 mm. How far from the axis does the 1st order lie in the focal plane? Check with the optical bench.

\(x_1 = \lambda f/p = 532\cdot10^{-6}\,\text{mm}\cdot 200\,\text{mm}/0.25\,\text{mm} \approx 0.43\,\text{mm}\).
2. Iris and wavelength

An iris with radius 0.30 mm sits in the focal plane (f = 200 mm). What is the smallest grating period that can be seen in the image with red light (650 nm) and with blue light (450 nm)?

\(p_{\min} = \lambda f/R\): red 0.43 mm, blue 0.30 mm. Blue light reproduces finer details.
3. The resolution of a telescope

A telescope has an aperture of 114 mm. What is the angular radius of the Airy disc at 550 nm, in arcseconds?

\(\theta = 1.22\,\lambda/D = 1.22\cdot 550\cdot10^{-9}/0.114 \approx 5.9\cdot10^{-6}\) rad ≈ 1.2″. In practice atmospheric turbulence (seeing) usually limits it to 2–3″.
4. The limit of the microscope

An objective has NA = 0.65. What is the smallest distance between two details that can be separated in green light (550 nm) with oblique illumination?

\(d = \lambda/(2\,\mathrm{NA}) = 550/1.3 \approx 420\) nm.
5. Phase contrast

A cell delays the light by a phase of 0.2 rad relative to its surroundings. How large is the relative contrast with Zernike's phase plate, and with dark field?

Phase contrast: \(I \approx 1 + 2\varphi = 1.4\), i.e. 40 % brighter than the background. Dark field: \(I \approx \varphi^2 = 0.04\) on a black background – faint, but on a dark ground it can be seen.
6. Why diagonal wires?

In the Abbe–Porter experiment you let only the diagonal row of spots through the focal plane. Explain why the image shows diagonal stripes, and what period they have when the period of the mesh is \(p\).

The diagonal row corresponds to the frequencies \(m(1/p, 1/p)\), i.e. a wave that varies along the diagonal with the frequency \(\sqrt2/p\). The stripes are perpendicular to the diagonal and have the period \(p/\sqrt2\).

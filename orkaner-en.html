The structure of the storm

windair pressure

A heat engine

The air takes up heat and water vapour at the sea temperature Ts and gives it off again at the top of the storm at the cold temperature To. It is a Carnot engine. Emanuel showed that the strongest wind a storm can reach is

Vmax² = (Ck/CD) · (Ts − To)/To · (k*s − k)

where k*s − k is the difference in heat content between saturated air at the sea surface and the air just above, set mostly by evaporation.

Six conditions for a storm to be born

A tropical cyclone does not arise from nothing. In 1968 the meteorologist William Gray found the conditions that are almost always met when a storm forms:

1. The sea is at least 26.5 °C warm, down to about 50 metres depth. 2. The place lies at least 5° from the equator, so the Coriolis force can set the air spinning. 3. The wind shear, the difference between the wind near the ground and at 12 km height, is small, below about 10 m/s. 4. The air at 5 km height is moist. 5. The atmosphere is unstable, so warm, moist air can rise. 6. There is a disturbance to start from, often an easterly wave in the trade winds from West Africa.

That is why the Atlantic hurricane season runs from June to November, peaking in September, when the sea is warmest. In the southern hemisphere the season runs from November to April. In the South Atlantic and the eastern Pacific off South America the water is too cold, and tropical cyclones almost never form.

Why it spins

As air flows in towards the low pressure in the centre, it is deflected by the Earth's rotation, to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. The air therefore circles the centre: anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the south. The Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin φ is zero at the equator, so no cyclones form within 5° of the equator, and none can cross it. The angular momentum of the vortex is conserved as the air flows in, and the wind grows stronger the closer it gets to the centre, like a skater pulling in her arms.

The eye and the eyewall

Close to the centre the air cannot get further in: the centrifugal and Coriolis forces balance the pressure. Instead it rises in the eyewall, a ring of towering thunderclouds where the wind is strongest. When the water vapour condenses, the latent heat is released, and the air becomes warm and light. In the middle the air sinks slowly, warms by compression and dissolves the clouds: the eye, 20–60 km across and often almost calm. The wind profile in the chart follows Holland's model for the selected storm.

A heat engine on the sea

The energy comes from the sea. The wind evaporates water from the sea surface, and the water vapour carries the latent heat up into the eyewall. The air flows in at the sea temperature, about 300 K, and out at 15 km height at about 200 K. The efficiency is (Ts − To)/Ts ≈ 1/3, like a Carnot engine, and the work is used to keep the wind going against friction at the sea surface. A large hurricane releases more than 10¹⁴ W as heat of condensation, many times the whole of humanity's energy use. See also the page on the Carnot cycle.

Why the storm travels

A tropical cyclone is carried by the wind around it, the steering flow. In the tropics the trade winds blow westward, and the storm moves west. On the western side of the subtropical high the flow turns towards the pole, and the storm turns with it. When it reaches the belt of westerlies, it swings east. This recurvature is why many Atlantic hurricanes end up off the east coast of the USA and carry on towards Europe as ordinary lows. In addition the storm drifts by itself a little towards the pole and west, at 1–3 m/s, because the Coriolis parameter grows towards the pole. This is called beta drift.

How the storm dies

Over land the storm loses its heat source and weakens within a day or two, but it can still bring torrential rain and floods. The same happens over cold water. The storm can also weaken itself: the strong wind mixes cold water up from the depths, especially if it moves slowly and the warm layer is thin. Try the Thickness of the warm surface layer slider. Strong wind shear tilts the storm and blows the warm, moist air away from the centre.

A warmer climate

A warmer sea gives a higher potential intensity. The UN climate panel, the IPCC, judges in its sixth report (2021) that the proportion of the strongest storms, categories 4 and 5, has likely increased, and that the rain from tropical cyclones is getting heavier, because warm air can hold about 7 % more water vapour per degree. Whether the total number of storms changes is more uncertain. Try the Warming of the sea slider.

What the model computes

The sea temperature is a simple climatology that varies with latitude, longitude and month. The potential intensity is computed with Emanuel's formula. The storm's wind and inner-core moisture follow Emanuel's simple model from 2017: the wind grows towards the potential intensity when the core is moist, while wind shear dries out the core, and the cooling of the sea under the storm holds it back. The storm is carried by a steering flow made of the trade winds, the westerlies, the flow round the subtropical highs and the beta drift.

Limitations

Sea temperature, wind shear and steering flow are smooth averages, not the weather on a particular day. Real tracks are steered by highs and lows that move, and forecasts use the whole atmosphere. Over land only a simple decay is computed. The storms are drawn much larger than they are.

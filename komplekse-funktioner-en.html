Complex variable theory – complex functions and residues

Complex numbers were invented in the sixteenth century to solve cubic equations and were long regarded as “imaginary”. Today they are indispensable in physics and engineering: alternating current, quantum mechanics, signal processing and the flow round a wing are all calculated with complex functions. Complex Variable Theory shows that a complex-differentiable function has remarkably rigid properties, and that integrals can be evaluated simply by counting poles. Complex numbers

A complex number $z = x + iy$ is a point in the plane. Multiplying by $i$ is a rotation by 90°, and in general lengths multiply and angles add: in polar form $z = re^{i\theta}$, $z_1z_2 = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$. The link is Euler’s formula

$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta,\qquad e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$$

which unites the exponential function with the trigonometric functions. That is why oscillations are so easy to handle with complex numbers: $e^{i\omega t}$ is a vector rotating with angular velocity $\omega$, and its real part is a cosine oscillation (see the phasors in circuit theory).

Analytic functions

A complex function $f(z) = u(x, y) + iv(x, y)$ is differentiable if the limit $f'(z) = \lim_{h\to0}(f(z+h) - f(z))/h$ is the same whatever direction $h$ approaches zero from. That is a strong requirement, and it is equivalent to the Cauchy–Riemann equations

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$

A function that is differentiable in a region is called analytic (holomorphic). It is then automatically differentiable infinitely many times and equal to its Taylor series. Both $u$ and $v$ satisfy Laplace’s equation $\nabla^2u = 0$ – which is why analytic functions can be used to solve two-dimensional problems in electrostatics, heat conduction and flow.

Functions in colour

A complex function maps a plane onto a plane and cannot be drawn as an ordinary graph – that would need four dimensions. An elegant solution is domain colouring: each point $z$ is coloured according to the value $f(z)$, with the hue given by the argument (angle) and the brightness by the magnitude.

Try it: The colour shows the argument of $f(z)$ – red for positive real values, green and blue round the circle – and the dark rings show where $|f(z)|$ doubles. Zeros appear as points where all the colours meet in one sense of rotation, poles as points where they meet in the opposite sense; a zero of order 2 has the colours twice round. With the grid switched on, the grid lines of the real and imaginary parts of $f$ appear: they cross at right angles wherever $f'(z) \neq 0$ – the map is conformal. For $\sqrt z$ there is a branch cut along the negative axis, where the colours jump.

Conformal maps

An analytic function preserves angles: small figures are rotated and scaled, but not distorted. That makes it possible to map a difficult region onto an easy one. The classic example is Nikolai Zhukovsky’s map $w = z + 1/z$ from 1910, which maps a circle onto an aerofoil. The flow round a cylinder is known exactly, and the map gives the flow round the wing at once – and the lift via the Kutta–Zhukovsky theorem $L = \rho U\Gamma$ (see the wind tunnel and fluid mechanics).

Cauchy’s theorem

Augustin-Louis Cauchy showed around 1825 that the integral of an analytic function round a closed curve is zero if the function is analytic everywhere inside the curve:

$$\oint_C f(z)\,dz = 0,\qquad f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{f(z)}{z - a}\,dz$$

The second formula, Cauchy’s integral formula, is even more surprising: the value of $f$ at an interior point is completely determined by its values on the boundary. It is the complex version of the mean-value property of harmonic functions (see applied field theory).

Residues

If $f$ has isolated singularities, it can be expanded near each of them in a Laurent series with negative powers, $f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_n(z - z_0)^n$. The coefficient $a_{-1}$ is the residue, and the residue theorem says that

$$\oint_C f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i\sum_k \mathrm{Res}(f, z_k)$$

summed over the singularities inside the curve. For a simple pole $\mathrm{Res}(f, z_0) = \lim_{z\to z_0}(z - z_0)f(z)$. With the theorem many real integrals can be evaluated that are otherwise impossible – for example $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{dx}{1+x^2} = \pi$, which comes from the residue at $z = i$ – and in physics it is used to compute Fourier transforms, Green’s functions and the Kramers–Kronig relations between refractive index and absorption.

The figure computes the integral $\oint f(z)\,dz$ numerically round the circle and compares it with $2\pi i$ times the sum of the residues of the poles (red crosses) that lie inside. Move the circle across a pole and watch the integral jump. If no poles lie inside, the integral is zero – Cauchy’s theorem. For $e^z/z^3$ the pole is of third order, and the residue is $\tfrac12$, the coefficient of $z^2$ in the Taylor series of $e^z$.

Applications

  • Electrical engineering: Impedances, transfer functions and stability. An amplifier with feedback is stable if the poles of its transfer function lie in the left half-plane – Nyquist’s criterion (1932) is built directly on the argument principle.
  • Flow and electrostatics: Two-dimensional potential problems are solved with complex potentials and conformal maps.
  • Quantum mechanics: The wave function is complex, and poles of scattering amplitudes in the complex energy plane correspond to bound states and resonances.
  • Acoustics: Sound propagation in a pipe with an absorbing lining has complex propagation constants, and the roots of the transcendental equation are found in the complex plane – exactly the problem in my thesis at DTH.

Since 1978

  • Fractals: The Mandelbrot set (1980) – the values of $c$ for which the iteration $z \mapsto z^2 + c$ stays bounded – made complex functions one of the best-known images of the age.
  • Computer graphics: Domain colouring only became practical with colour screens and is now the preferred way to show complex functions.
  • The Riemann hypothesis on the zeros of the zeta function $\zeta(s)$ is still unproved and one of the Clay Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems from 2000.

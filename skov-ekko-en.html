The echo of the forest

Clap your hands in a forest, and every trunk sends a small piece of the clap back. In a random natural forest they merge into a hissing tail. In a plantation, where the trees stand in rows, the echo becomes a regular train of echoes, almost a tone, in the same way as X-rays give sharp spots when they are scattered in a crystal.

The physics behind it

The echo from one trunk

A trunk is a vertical cylinder with radius \(a\). When the clap hits it at the distance \(r\), the sound is thrown back from the part of the trunk facing you. Vertically the trunk acts as a plane mirror, horizontally as a curved mirror that spreads the sound. Ray tracing gives

\[p_\text{echo} \approx \frac{p_0}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{a}{2r}}, \qquad E_\text{echo} \propto \frac{a}{r^3}.\]

This holds when the wavelength is shorter than the circumference of the trunk (\(ka \gg 1\)). For low notes (\(ka < 1\)) the sound slides around the trunk, and the echo is weakened as \((ka)^{3/2}\) in amplitude. The transition lies at \(ka = 1\), i.e. \(f = c/2\pi a\). A trunk with a diameter of \(30\ \text{cm}\) (\(a = 0.15\ \text{m}\)) therefore reflects most above \(f \approx 360\ \text{Hz}\). The energy of the clap lies around \(1\text{–}2\ \text{kHz}\), so that fits nicely.

The height of the tree hardly matters. The part of the trunk that contributes is a Fresnel zone with a height of about \(\sqrt{\lambda r}\). For \(f = 1\ \text{kHz}\), \(\lambda \approx 0.34\ \text{m}\), and at the distance \(r = 20\ \text{m}\) this becomes \(\sqrt{0.34 \cdot 20}\ \text{m} \approx 2.6\ \text{m}\). The trunk is almost always taller than that. That is why the slider for tree height has been removed and replaced by the trunk thickness, which matters a lot.

Why the echo is so weak, and why it is amplified

Compare the echo from one trunk with the clap at your own ears. The hands are about \(r_0 = 0.4\ \text{m}\) from the ears, so the clap has the amplitude \(p_0/r_0\). The echo from a trunk at the distance \(r\) is \((p_0/2r)\sqrt{a/2r}\). The ratio is

\[\frac{p_\text{echo}}{p_\text{clap}} = \frac{r_0}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{a}{2r}} .\]

For a trunk with radius \(a = 0.15\ \text{m}\) at the distance \(r = 20\ \text{m}\) this gives

\[\frac{p_\text{echo}}{p_\text{clap}} = \frac{0.4}{40}\sqrt{\frac{0.15}{40}} \approx 6.1\cdot10^{-4}.\]

In decibels a difference in sound pressure is \(\Delta L = 20\log_{10}(p_1/p_2)\), so the echo lies \(20\log_{10}(6.1\cdot10^{-4}) \approx -64\ \text{dB}\) below the clap. If you stand in the meadow, the echo of the whole forest edge is typically \(50\text{–}70\ \text{dB}\) weaker than the clap. Inside the forest, where the nearest trunks stand a few metres away, the difference is smaller, about \(50\ \text{dB}\).

Even so, you can clearly hear the echo of the forest in reality. A clap can give over \(100\ \text{dB}\) at the ear, so the echo is still at \(40\text{–}50\ \text{dB}\), and in a quiet forest the background noise is perhaps \(25\text{–}30\ \text{dB}\). The ear has a dynamic range of about \(120\ \text{dB}\) and recovers from the clap within a few tenths of a second.

A loudspeaker cannot do the same. In an ordinary living room, and especially from a phone, there are only \(40\text{–}60\ \text{dB}\) between the loudest sound and what can just be distinguished from the noise in the room. If you play the clap at full strength, the echo therefore disappears completely. That was the reason why the echo was at first almost inaudible on this page.

That is why the echo is amplified in playback, so that the strongest echo lies \(12\ \text{dB}\) below the clap. The page shows how many dB it has been amplified. All echoes are multiplied by the same factor, so the ratios between them are unchanged: the order, the intervals between the row echoes in the plantation and the fall as \(t^{-2}\) are the same as without amplification. Only the ratio to the clap has changed. It is like stretching an astrophoto so that a faint galaxy can be seen next to a bright star.

Turn off the amplification, and you hear the true strength ratios. Choose „Remove the direct clap“, and you hear the echo alone, almost as you do in reality once the ear has recovered from the clap. The graphs always show the true, unamplified values.

Why the energy falls as \(t^{-2}\)

An echo that returns after the time \(t\) comes from trees at the distance \(r = ct/2\). If the trees stand randomly with the density \(n\) per m², the number of trees in a ring of width \(dr\) equals \(2\pi n r\,dr\). This grows with \(t\). Each echo has the energy \(\propto a/r^3 \propto t^{-3}\). Altogether:

\[E(t)\,dt \;\propto\; \underbrace{t\,dt}_{\text{number of trees}} \cdot \underbrace{t^{-3}}_{\text{per tree}} \;=\; t^{-2}\,dt .\]

So the power law comes from the geometry: a two-dimensional forest of tall cylinders. Spherical scatterers in three-dimensional space would give \(t^{-3}\) instead. The echogram shows that the calculated sound follows \(t^{-2}\) until the attenuation through the forest takes over.

An earlier version of the page explained \(t^{-2}\) by absorption in the forest floor and sound disappearing up through the crowns. Those two things make the echo weaker, but they are not what gives the exponent \(-2\).

The effective wavenumber: the forest as a medium

The sound that reaches the distant trees has itself been weakened by the nearer trees, because they have scattered some of it away. This is described with a complex effective wavenumber. For a dilute medium of scatterers (Foldy):

\[k_\text{eff} \approx k + \frac{2\pi n}{k} f(0), \qquad \operatorname{Im} k_\text{eff} = \frac{n\,\sigma_\text{ext}}{2},\]

where \(f(0)\) is the scattering amplitude straight ahead. The second equation is the optical theorem: the imaginary part of the forward amplitude tells how much energy is removed from the direct wave. For a thick trunk the extinction width is \(\sigma_\text{ext} \approx 4a\), twice the trunk's shadow (the extinction paradox). The mean free path is \(\ell = 1/(n\sigma_\text{ext})\). The echo is weakened by \(e^{-r/\ell}\) in amplitude on the way out and back, and that is the grey curve in the echogram.

When the sound has travelled many free paths, the motion from tree to tree becomes a kind of random walk (a Wiener process), and the sound diffuses. In an ordinary forest typically \(\ell \approx 30\text{–}100\ \text{m}\), so the first tenths of a second of the echo are dominated by single scattering, as here.

The plantation as a crystal: Bragg echo

Place the trees in a square lattice with the spacing \(d\). The rows perpendicular to your line of sight lie at the distances \(d, 2d, 3d,\dots\), so their echoes arrive with the fixed time interval \(2d/c\). A regular train of echoes is heard as a tone with the fundamental frequency \(c/2d\) and all its overtones. This is Bragg's law for backscattering (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)):

\[2d\sin\theta = m\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_m = m\,\frac{c}{2d}.\]

Other families of rows give their own combs: the diagonal ones with \(d/\sqrt2\), the next with \(d/\sqrt5\) and so on, exactly like the lattice planes \((hk)\) in a crystal with \(d_{hk} = d/\sqrt{h^2+k^2}\). The same phenomenon is heard at a picket fence or a long staircase. The best-known staircase is the pyramid at Chichén Itzá, where a clap gives a chirping echo.

Stand in the meadow in front of the plantation and clap. Each row sends an echo back, and in the panel „The echo in time“ they land exactly on the dashed lines with the spacing \(2d/c\). The ear hears it as a buzzing, repeated sound with the fundamental \(c/2d\). But look closely: after each row echo follows a small tail of weaker echoes. They come from the trunks further out to the side in the same row, which are a little further away. A row of individual trunks is not a plane mirror for a nearby sound source; only the nearest trunks lie within the Fresnel zone. That is why the Bragg comb in the spectrum is blurred, and the tone is only faint in a real plantation.

X-rays have it easier: the source is far away, so the wave is plane, and each lattice plane contains millions of atoms. That is why the Bragg spots from a crystal are so sharp. In the middle of the plantation the effect is even weaker, because the two or three nearest trunks completely dominate the echo.

Disorder and the Debye–Waller factor

In a crystal the atoms vibrate about their positions. A snapshot shows them displaced by a random distance \(u\). The scattered wave from each atom thereby gets a random phase \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{u}\), and the coherent part, the Bragg peak, is weakened by the Debye–Waller factor

\[I = I_0\,e^{-2W}, \qquad 2W = q^2\langle u^2\rangle .\]

For backscattering \(q = 2k\). If the plantation's trees are moved randomly with the spread \(\sigma\), \(2W = 4k^2\sigma^2\). High orders, and thus high frequencies, disappear first, exactly as the outermost X-ray spots fade when the crystal is heated. The energy is not lost; it becomes diffuse noise between the peaks, corresponding to thermal diffuse scattering. Try slowly turning up the disorder and look at the panel „The echo in time“: the row echoes slide away from the dashed lines and smear out. Just a few per cent disorder is enough, because the frequencies in a clap are high. This also explains why a real plantation rarely rings clearly: trees are not planted with centimetre accuracy.

The original page compared the natural forest with a crystal whose atoms vibrate. That is not quite right. A natural forest has no rows at all; it corresponds to an amorphous substance such as glass or a liquid. Here X-ray scattering gives no sharp spots, only broad, diffuse rings, because the atoms cannot come closer than a certain distance. Trees likewise have a minimum distance, so the natural forest also has a little short-range order. The Debye–Waller picture, on the other hand, fits a plantation with slightly crookedly planted trees exactly.

One more difference: X-rays are scattered weakly, so the crystal can be described with single scattering. In a dense forest the sound can be scattered several times. The model here only includes single scattering plus the average attenuation.

The limits of the model

The trunks are rigid, vertical cylinders. Crowns, branches, leaves and the forest floor are not included, nor are wind and temperature layers. The air absorption corresponds to \(20\ ^\circ\text{C}\) and \(50\ \%\) relative humidity. Direction is heard through a simple time difference and loudness difference between the ears; you face the top of the map.

The physics behind it

Bending waves in a plate floating on water

When the stone strikes, the ice bends locally, and the bending runs outwards as a wave. The ice is a thin plate with flexural rigidity

\[D = \frac{E\,h^3}{12\,(1-\nu^2)},\]

where \(E \approx 9\ \text{GPa}\) is Young’s modulus, \(h\) the thickness and \(\nu \approx 0.33\) Poisson’s ratio. The plate rests on water, which has to move with it when the ice bends. The water acts as an extra mass per unit area of \(\rho_w/k\), large for long waves and small for short ones. Together with gravity this gives the dispersion relation

\[\omega^2 = \frac{D\,k^5 + \rho_w g\,k}{\rho_i h\,k + \rho_w}.\]

At very high frequencies thin-plate theory stops being valid, because the wavelength approaches the thickness of the ice. The wave then cannot travel faster than a surface wave (a Rayleigh wave, \(c_R \approx 1800\ \text{m/s}\) in ice). The simulation allows for this by adding a wavenumber \(\omega/c_R\) in quadrature. This is an approximation to Mindlin plate theory, but it gives the right limit.

Why the note falls: the group velocity

The energy in a wave travels at the group velocity \(v_g = d\omega/dk\). For a free thin plate \(\omega \propto k^2\), so

\[v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \sqrt{\omega}\left(\frac{D}{\rho_i h}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad v_g = 2\,v_p .\]

The frequency \(f\) reaches distance \(r\) at time \(t = r/v_g(f)\). Since \(v_g \propto \sqrt{f}\), we get \(f \propto r^2/t^2\): the high notes arrive first, then the pitch slides quickly downwards. That is the characteristic “pew” sound. The further away the stone is, the more the arc is stretched out in time. Try moving further away with the slider and hear how “tick” turns into “pew”. The yellow curve in the spectrogram is exactly \(t = r/v_g(f)\) for the first bounce, and it lies right on top of the calculated sound.

The sound is calculated by adding all the frequencies together, each with its own phase delay \(e^{-ik(\omega)r}\), and then taking an inverse Fourier transform. So the falling pitch is not drawn in as a curve; it emerges by itself.

Critical frequency: when the ice radiates sound

For the wave in the ice to send sound up into the air, the sound wave in the air must match the wave in the ice along the surface. The vertical wavenumber in the air is

\[k_z = \sqrt{k_0^2 - k^2}, \qquad k_0 = \omega / c_\text{air}.\]

If the wave in the ice is faster than sound in air, then \(k < k_0\), \(k_z\) is real, and the sound radiates upwards at an angle. If it is slower, \(k_z\) becomes imaginary and the pressure falls off as \(e^{-\alpha z}\) with height. This is called an evanescent wave; it does not reach your ear at height \(z = 1.6\ \text{m}\). The boundary is the critical frequency, which for a thin plate is

\[f_c \approx \frac{c_\text{air}^2}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_i h}{D}} .\]

For a free plate it is inversely proportional to the thickness: \(f_c \approx 1800\ \text{Hz}\) for \(h = 1\ \text{cm}\) and \(f_c \approx 180\ \text{Hz}\) for \(h = 10\ \text{cm}\). The water under the ice, however, slows the waves down, and this raises the critical frequency noticeably, most of all for thin ice: with the water included it becomes \(f_c \approx 3\ \text{kHz}\) for \(h = 1\ \text{cm}\) and \(f_c \approx 300\ \text{Hz}\) for \(h = 10\ \text{cm}\). The exact value is shown in the panel. Thin ice therefore sings loud and clear. Below \(f_c\) you only hear what cracks, snow, bubbles and the shore scatter up into the air. This is controlled by the slider “Cracks, snow and shores”.

The stone’s bounces and the click in the air

The stone hits the ice with a vertical speed \(v_z\) and bounces off again with a fraction \(e\) of it (the coefficient of restitution). The time in the air is \(2v_z/g\), so the bounces come closer and closer together until the stone simply slides. While it slides, small bumps give many weak impacts, each sending off its own small arc.

The contact time of an impact follows Hertz contact theory, \(\tau \propto m^{2/5} v_z^{-1/5}\). A short impact contains high frequencies; a long one (a heavy stone) mostly low ones. The impact also sends a short click directly through the air at the speed of sound. When the stone is far away, the click arrives after the fast waves in the ice.

You might think the Doppler effect changes the pitch, because the stone is moving. But each impact lasts less than a millisecond, and the note is made by the ice, not by the stone. The stone’s motion shows only in the rhythm, in the time between the bounces as you hear them, and in the fact that each bounce happens at a new distance from you, so the arcs have different lengths.

Limits of the model

The ice is taken to be infinitely large and uniform, with no reflections from the shore. The water is taken to be infinitely deep. Damping in the ice is described by a loss factor that grows when the ice is close to \(0\ ^\circ\text{C}\). The sound pressure at the ear is calculated from the velocity of the plate beneath the listener; the direction of the sound is not included. The result is therefore a qualitatively correct sound with the right timing and frequencies, not a recording from a particular lake.

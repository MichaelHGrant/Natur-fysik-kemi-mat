Comet 161P: orbit determination

Four evenings with an eVscope2 in Djursland, a plate solution against Tycho-2 and Gauss’s method – and why the length of the arc and a single centring decide the result.

Michael Howard Grant Senior lecturer emeritus · Grenaa Gymnasium · updated 4 November 2026
161P/Hartley–IRAS · 3 Nov 2026 · eVscope2
01 Summary

The main results

Four eVscope2 images from 18, 19 and 26 October and 3 November 2026 were plate-solved against Tycho-2 with 25–49 reference stars and a residual scatter of 1.0–1.4″. Across the comet’s track the positions lie 0.4–3″ from the MPC orbit on three of the nights; on 26 October the offset is 13″.

With the three October images alone, Gauss’s method gave a distance 19 % too small and e = 0.41 against 0.836. The image from 3 November doubles the arc to 16 days. The nights of 18 and 19 October and 3 November now give a distance only 7 % too small and e = 0.62.

With 26 October as the middle night the solution is very stable, but it lands 10 % too far out, because the comet’s centre that evening is measured 13″ off. Remove those 13″ and Gauss gives e = 0.852, q = 1.270 AU and i = 96.1° – almost exactly the MPC orbit.

02 The observations

Four evenings in October and November

All four images are eVscope2 stacks from Djursland, Denmark (56.5° N, 10.8° E), saved as the app’s annotated 2800 × 2800 pixel export. For the October images the time in the file name (UTC) is taken to be the end of the stack, so the mid-time is the file name minus half the integration time.

The image from 3 November came without a file name. Its time is taken from the image’s EXIF data (17:43 UTC) plus half a minute. On the October images the EXIF time was within 0–2 minutes of the middle of the stack.

In October the comet is a diffuse, bluish coma about 1′ across. On 26 October four stars lie on top of the coma, which makes centring harder. On 3 November it is clearly cyan: the greenish gas glow of C₂ grows as the comet approaches the Sun.

03 Astrometry

Plate solution against Tycho-2

The coordinates in the app’s file names cannot be used: on 18 and 19 October they lie 0.7–1.0° from the comet, outside the field of view. All positions were therefore measured with an independent plate solution against Tycho-2 (the Big Sky catalogue), with the stars’ proper motions carried forward to 2026.8.

Find the stars

DAOStarFinder finds 850–3300 stars in each image, out to a radius of 0.44 of the image width, which avoids the text around the edge.

Match the pattern

The brightest stars are matched against the catalogue by searching over scale, rotation and offset. The scale came out at 0.849″ per pixel on all four nights.

Fit the transformation

A six-parameter affine transformation is fitted by least squares to 25–49 stars. The residual scatter is 1.0–1.4″.

Measure the coma

Stars are masked and an elliptical 2D Gaussian with background is fitted to the coma. On 3 November the blue-minus-red channel is used, so the red halo of a neighbouring star does not pull the centre aside.

04 Check

Measured against computed: O − C

The measured positions were compared with a topocentric ephemeris computed from the MPC orbital elements (MPEC 2026-TP0, epoch 23 October 2026), with light-time correction. The difference O − C is split into components along and across the comet’s motion.

  • Across the track the difference is 0.4″, 3.1″ and 0.6″ on 18 and 19 October and 3 November. That matches the accuracy of the plate solution and shows that the astrometry is sound.
  • Along the track the comet lags by 28″, 17″, 7″ and 8″. Given the comet’s rate of motion, the effective times are 7.4, 4.7, 2.9 and 5.5 minutes earlier than assumed. It is the time stamp of the stack, not the measurement, that is uncertain.
  • On 26 October there is a 13″ offset across the track. It is probably caused by the stars on top of the coma or by an asymmetric coma; it persists with four different centring methods, including the blue channel alone.
05 The method

Gauss’s method

Gauss’s method finds the comet’s distance ρ at the three times from the direction vectors L̂i and the observer’s heliocentric position Ri (the Earth plus the geocentric position of Marshøj). The middle distance r2 from the Sun is a root of Gauss’s equation of eighth degree:

r28 − (A2 + 2AE + R22) r26 − 2μB(A + E) r23 − μ2B2 = 0,   E = L̂2 · R2

Lagrange’s f and g functions are then refined with universal variables and light-time correction until the solution stops changing. The velocity follows from

v2 = (−f3 r1 + f1 r3) / (f1g3 − f3g1)

The code was checked by feeding it the MPC ephemeris’ own directions at the same times. It recovers the MPC orbit to every decimal (q = 1.26511 AU, e = 0.83618, i = 95.7919°).

07 Uncertainty

Why 8 days is not enough

A Monte Carlo run with 3000 repetitions, shaking each position by ±2″ and each time by ±4 min, gives ρ2 = 0.44–0.63 AU and e = 0.39–0.59 (68 % intervals). The true ρ2 = 0.692 AU lies just outside the interval, because the systematic 13″ offset on 26 October is larger than the assumed 2″.

The right panel is a simulation with perfect MPC positions plus ±2″ and ±1 min. Even with data that good, 8 days give e = 0.55–1.19, so an ellipse cannot be told from a hyperbola. A 16-day arc (an image around 3 November) gives 0.75–0.92 and ρ2 within ±2.5 %. That image arrived – see the next section.

105″ARCSECONDS FROM THE GREAT CIRCLE

In practice Gauss’s method measures the curvature of the track. According to the MPC orbit, the position on 19 October lies only 100″ from the great circle through the other two, and according to my measurements 105″. Those 5″ are what shift the distance by 19 %.

08 Fourth night

  • 18 · 19 Oct · 3 Nov avoids the bad night. The distance is 7 % too small, and the MPC value sits right at the edge of the 68 % interval. But two nights close together make a weak middle point, so the spread is large.
  • 18 · 26 Oct · 3 Nov has a well-placed middle night, and the spread is only ±0.013 AU. Timing errors of ±4 min hardly matter over 16 days. Instead, the 13″ on 26 October comes straight through: the distance is 10 % too large and the orbit becomes a hyperbola.
  • The check removes the 13″ across the track on 26 October. Gauss then lands almost exactly on the MPC orbit. It is not an independent measurement, since the correction is taken from the MPC orbit, but it shows that the method works and that the result hinges on one centring.
13″ONE CENTRING, 10 % OF THE DISTANCE

With a 16-day arc the times are no longer the problem. It is the precision of the middle point across the track that fixes the distance, and 13″ on 26 October shifts it by 10 %.

09 Conclusion

Next steps

The eVscope2 astrometry is good enough for orbit determination: the plate solutions hold to 1–1.4″, and the positions are only 0.4–3″ off across the track on three of four nights. With a 16-day arc Gauss’s method works; the remaining error comes from one poor centring on 26 October.

  • Take an image around 12 November, preferably several in a row. The comet will then be at RA 19h 32m, Dec +16.6°, 73° from the Sun. With five nights the orbit can be fitted by least squares over all points, and a single bad point will give itself away.
  • Avoid fields where stars cover the coma, or take two stacks half an hour apart so that the comet has moved off the stars.
  • Download the raw data from the app (manifest.json with obs_start and obs_end). The stack’s mid-time is then known to the second. With a long arc it matters less, but it removes the 7–28″ offset along the track.
  • Use the EXIF time when the file name is missing: on these images it was within 2 minutes of the middle of the stack.

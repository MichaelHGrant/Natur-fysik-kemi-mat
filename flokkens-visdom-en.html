The Wisdom of the Crowd

Many imperfect measurements, averaged together, can beat any single one of them. Here are two examples I used in my teaching: a class measuring the Earth the way Eratosthenes did, but with the GPS in their phones, and the clock room of a ship finding its longitude by taking the average of many chronometers.

Eratosthenes with GPS

I had my pupils walk a few hundred metres from north to south and note the GPS coordinates at the start and at the finish. One pupil's readings are not precise enough, but when we took the average for the whole class, we could repeat Eratosthenes' measurement and find the circumference of the Earth. What Eratosthenes did

Around 240 BC Eratosthenes, librarian at Alexandria, learnt that at noon on midsummer's day the Sun stood directly overhead at Syene (today's Aswan) in southern Egypt. At the same moment in Alexandria, further north, a vertical stick cast a shadow: the Sun was about 7.2° from the vertical, one fiftieth of a full circle. The distance between the two cities was about 5000 stadia, so the circumference of the Earth had to be fifty times as much, about 250 000 stadia.

The idea is simple: if two places on the same meridian are a distance \(d\) apart and their latitudes differ by \(\Delta\varphi\), then

\[C = d \cdot \frac{360^\circ}{\Delta\varphi}.\]

Eratosthenes found \(\Delta\varphi\) from the shadow of a stick. The pupils read it straight off the GPS.

Why one pupil is not enough

A phone's GPS position is typically uncertain by a few metres. Over a walk of 300 m, a 5 m error in each of the two readings gives an uncertainty in the measured north–south distance of about \(\sqrt{2}\cdot 5\ \text{m} \approx 7\ \text{m}\), or more than 2 %. That is roughly ±1000 km on the circumference of the Earth.

The errors of different pupils are independent: some are too large and some too small. When we average \(N\) results, the random errors largely cancel, and the uncertainty of the average falls as

\[\sigma_{\text{average}} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{one}}}{\sqrt{N}}.\]

With 25 pupils the uncertainty is five times smaller than for one pupil. Try moving the slider for the number of pupils and watch the yellow line settle down.

The wisdom of the crowd

In 1907 Francis Galton described a competition at a country fair in Plymouth, where about 800 visitors guessed the weight of an ox. Few guesses were close, but the middle value of all of them was within about 1 % of the true weight. This is the wisdom of the crowd: when the errors are independent and not all biased the same way, the crowd as a whole knows more than its members.

The GPS exercise lets pupils see this happen with their own measurements. It also shows the limit: averaging removes random errors, but not a systematic one. If every pupil walked slightly south-west instead of due south, or the distance on the map were wrong, the average would be wrong too.

The ship's clock room

The same idea kept time at sea. To find a ship's longitude, the navigator needed to know the time at Greenwich. A ship on a survey voyage carried many chronometers. Each day, at the same time, they were wound in the same order. They were never adjusted, only rewound and their readings noted. Some clocks gained and some lost, but the average of them all stayed very close to Greenwich time.

Time and longitude

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, or 15° an hour. If the Sun is highest in the sky (local noon) when a chronometer showing Greenwich time reads 14:00, the ship is two hours, or 30°, west of Greenwich. One second of time corresponds to 15 seconds of arc of longitude, which at the equator is about 460 m:

\[\Delta\lambda = 15^\circ\ \text{per hour} \times \Delta t, \qquad 1\ \text{s} \;\rightarrow\; 15'' \;\approx\; 0.46\ \text{km at the equator}.\]

A clock that is one minute wrong puts the ship 28 km out at the equator.

The chronometers of HMS Beagle

On the voyage of 1831–36, the one with Charles Darwin on board, the Beagle carried 22 chronometers in a cabin of their own. They were all wound at the same time every day and compared with one another, but never reset. Each clock's daily gain or loss, its rate, was recorded and allowed for, and the longitude was found from the mean of them all. The clocks were re-rated over several days at each harbour along the way.

When the Beagle had sailed right round the world, the sum of all the time differences should have been exactly 24 hours. It came to 24 hours and 33 seconds, an error of only about 15 km after five years at sea.

What averaging can and cannot do

Averaging \(N\) clocks reduces the random part of the error by a factor \(\sqrt{N}\), just as with the pupils' GPS readings. But a systematic error does not average out: if every clock ran slow in the cold, so would their mean. That is why each clock's rate was measured before departure and checked again at every port. In the simulation you can switch this correction off and on and see the difference.

Both examples rest on the same idea: many independent, imperfect measurements, averaged together, give a better answer than any single one. With a simulation, pupils can explore that idea for themselves, add more pupils or more clocks and see the uncertainty shrink, which a printed page alone cannot do.

The Polarisation Laboratory

The physics behind the three cases

1) Filters only (no plastic): When the light passes the first filter, it becomes polarised in one direction. The second filter (the analyser) blocks the light depending on the angle. This is described by Malus's law: $I = I_0 \cos^2(\theta)$. At exactly 90 degrees all light is blocked (crossed polarisers), and the field becomes completely black.

2) Filters with a piece of plastic (internal stresses): Ordinary transparent plastic (such as a plastic box or a ruler) often has non-uniform internal stresses from the moulding. This makes the material locally birefringent (photoelasticity). Because the thickness and the stress vary, it creates a complex, rainbow-coloured interference pattern that changes irregularly as you rotate the filter.

3) Filters with cling film (uniform orientation): Cling film is stretched during production, which forces all the long polymer molecules to point in the same direction. This makes the whole surface uniformly birefringent – it acts as an optical wave plate. It retards a particular wavelength uniformly over the whole surface. When you turn the filter, the interference switches between two fixed colours, often complementary colours (e.g. blue and reddish/orange).

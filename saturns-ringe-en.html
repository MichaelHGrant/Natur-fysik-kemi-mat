Saturn's Rings – birth, life and death

Saturn's rings may be far younger than Saturn itself – and they are disappearing. Follow in 3D how an icy moon may have been torn apart at the Roche limit, how the fragments were wound up into a thin, flat disc, and how "ring rain" along the magnetic field slowly drains the rings again.

About the model: the time axis is compressed and non-linear – the break-up itself takes days, the winding-up years, and the ring rain hundreds of millions of years. The thickness of the ring and the sizes of the moons and particles are greatly enlarged, and orbital speeds are slowed down; but the ratios between them follow Kepler's third law (T ∝ r3/2), so the inner particles are constantly overtaking the outer ones. The formation scenario is one of several (see below).

The rings in numbers

The rings consist of billions of particles, from dust grains to house-sized blocks of ice, each orbiting Saturn according to Kepler's laws. More than 95 % is water ice. The main rings (C, B and A) span about 62 000 km but are typically only around 10 m thick – relatively far thinner than a sheet of paper: a sheet 0.1 mm thick would have to be more than 600 m wide.

The Cassini spacecraft weighed the rings during its final orbits in 2017 by flying between the planet and the rings: about 1.5·1019 kg, or about 40 % of the moon Mimas. Packed together into a single ball of ice, the rings would be only about 320 km across.

Saturn rotates in 10 h 34 min. Particles inside the synchronous orbit (about 112 000 km, in the middle of the B ring) orbit faster than the planet turns; particles outside it more slowly.

The Roche limit: why there is a ring and not a moon

Saturn pulls harder on the side of a moon facing the planet than on the side facing away. The difference – the tidal force – tries to pull the moon apart. The moon's own gravity holds it together. Close enough to Saturn, the tidal force wins.

A simple estimate: a moon of radius a and mass m at distance d from a planet of mass M. The difference in Saturn's gravitational acceleration across the moon's radius is about 2GMa/d³, while the moon's own surface gravity is Gm/a². Set them equal and express the masses through densities:

d = 1.26 · Rp · (ρp / ρm)1/3

This holds for a rigid body. A fluid or loosely bound moon deforms and becomes more vulnerable; in 1849 Édouard Roche found the factor 2.44:

d = 2.44 · Rp · (ρp / ρm)1/3

Note that the size of the moon does not appear – only the densities. For Saturn (ρ = 687 kg/m³) and ice (917 kg/m³) the two limits lie at about 69 000 and 134 000 km. That is strikingly close to the inner and outer edges of the rings! Inside the Roche limit, pieces of ice cannot clump together into a moon – the ring stays a ring. Just outside it we find precisely the small moons Pan, Atlas, Prometheus and Pandora.

How did the rings form?

Nobody knows for certain. Any model has to explain that the rings are so pure (more than 95 % water ice, whereas the Solar System's icy moons are typically half rock), that they lie inside the Roche limit, and that they have the mass they have. The main proposals:

A layered moon is stripped (Canup 2010)

A large moon with a rocky core and a thick icy mantle migrates towards Saturn. At the Roche limit the loose icy mantle is stripped off and forms a massive ring of ice, while the dense rocky core continues inwards and plunges into the planet. That explains the pure ice. This is the sequence the animation shows.

The lost moon "Chrysalis" (Wisdom et al. 2022)

Saturn's axis is tilted by 26.7°. One model explains this through a resonance with Neptune's orbit, kept going by an extra moon. About 160 million years ago this moon's orbit became unstable; it came too close to Saturn and was torn apart – and the same event produced both the present tilt and young rings.

A collision between moons (Teodoro, Kegerreis et al. 2023)

Supercomputer simulations show that a collision between two mid-sized icy moons can send ice-rich material inside the Roche limit, where it forms a pure ring of ice – while the rock prefers to stay in the new moons outside.

Or: old rings

The classical view is that the rings are as old as Saturn (about 4.5 billion years), left over from the disc around the young planet or from an early, disrupted moon. Then there must be some mechanism that keeps the ice clean despite billions of years of meteoritic dust.

How old are the rings?

Meteoritic dust contains silicates and carbon and would darken and "dirty" the pure ice over time. Cassini's measurements of how much dust falls in (Kempf et al. 2023), the mass of the rings (Iess et al. 2019) and their purity point to an age of at most a few hundred million years – the rings would then have formed while the dinosaurs were alive. But the question is not settled: Hyodo, Genda and Madeira (2024) have argued from simulations that high-speed micrometeoroids vaporise and largely escape from the rings rather than polluting them. In that case the rings could well be old, even though they look young.

The structure of the rings

Resonances. When a ring particle and a moon have orbital periods in a simple integer ratio, the particle receives a small nudge at the same point in its orbit again and again. The nudges add up and shift the particle. The inner edge of the Cassini Division – the outer edge of the B ring – lies where particles orbit exactly twice for each orbit of Mimas. From Kepler's third law this happens at a distance of 185 539 km · (1/2)2/3 ≈ 117 000 km.

Small moons in the rings. Pan (about 28 km) keeps the Encke Gap in the A ring open, and Daphnis (about 8 km) the Keeler Gap. The shepherd moons Prometheus and Pandora on either side of the narrow F ring keep it confined.

Moons born from the rings. The ring slowly spreads, because the particles collide and exchange angular momentum: some moves inwards, some outwards. Material spreading beyond the Roche limit can clump together. Charnoz et al. (2010) showed that the small moons just outside the rings – Pan, Daphnis, Atlas, Prometheus, Pandora – very likely formed in exactly this way.

The spokes in the B ring. Dark radial "spokes" are sometimes seen across the B ring, made of fine, electrically charged dust hovering above the ring plane. They follow the planet's rotation rather than the Keplerian orbits and are linked to the magnetic field – the same physics as in the ring rain.

Ring rain – the rings are disappearing

Sunlight and meteoroid impacts give the finest ice grains an electric charge. A charged grain feels not only gravity but also Saturn's magnetic field, which is an almost perfect dipole aligned with the rotation axis. Along a dipole field line, r = L·cos²λ, where λ is the latitude and L the distance at which the line crosses the equatorial plane. The grains slide along the field lines and rain down into the atmosphere at the latitude where r = 1 RS, i.e. cos²λ = 1/L: for the C and B rings about 25–45°. That is exactly where the Keck telescope has seen a darker band in Saturn's ionosphere (O'Donoghue et al. 2013, 2019): the ring rain quenches the glow of the ionosphere.

From Earth-based observations the ring rain is estimated at 430–2 870 kg/s. During its "Grand Finale" in 2017, Cassini also measured a much larger flow – up to several tonnes per second – of ice, methane and organic compounds from the innermost D ring straight in towards the equator (Waite et al. 2018). Whether that flow is steady or a short-lived outburst is not known.

If the loss continues, the rings could be gone in a few hundred million years – short compared with Saturn's age of 4.5 billion years. If the rings are young, we are living in the small fraction of Solar System history in which Saturn has its great, bright rings. Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune have only faint rings today; perhaps they once had splendid rings like Saturn's.

Calculate it yourself: how long will the rings last?

Simple estimate: τ = M/ṁ with constant mass loss. In reality the loss changes as the rings become lighter and more spread out. An Olympic swimming pool holds about 2 500 tonnes of water.

The rings also vanish every 15 years – almost

Saturn's axis is tilted by 26.7°, and the rings lie in the equatorial plane. During Saturn's 29.5-year orbit we therefore see alternately the north and the south face of the rings, and twice per orbit we see them exactly edge-on. Because the rings are only a few metres to a kilometre thick, they then almost vanish in a telescope. That last happened on 23 March 2025, shortly before Saturn's equinox in May 2025, when the Sun changed sides on the rings. The next time is in 2038–39. Experiments and questions for the classroom

  1. Use the Roche calculator: how close can the Moon come to the Earth before it is torn apart? What if the Moon were made of ice?
  2. Run the animation slowly through stage 3. Why does a clump of fragments become a ring and not just a clump in orbit? (Hint: Kepler's third law.)
  3. Why does the disc become flat? Which quantity is conserved in the collisions, and which is lost?
  4. Calculate the orbital period at the inner and outer edges of the rings. How many times does a particle at the inner edge of the C ring orbit while one at the outer edge of the A ring orbits once?
  5. Use the dipole formula r = L·cos²λ to find the latitude at which ring rain from the Cassini Division (L ≈ 2.0) strikes Saturn.
  6. Use the lifetime calculator with 430 kg/s and with 10 000 kg/s. How large is the uncertainty?

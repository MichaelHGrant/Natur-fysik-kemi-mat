The arrow of time

From Newton's reversible mechanics to Leggett's question of how information disappears into Heisenberg's shadows.

Michael Howard Grant retired teacher · Grenaa Gymnasium
1978 SUSSEX
Institutional context

Based on the syllabus at my alma mater, the University of Sussex, School of Mathematical and Physical Sciences, in the 1970s and 80s — and my BSc final-year project from 1978.

How does irreversibility — the direction of time, the arrow of time — arise in a universe governed by reversible laws? It is one of the deepest riddles in physics.

This article traces the development from the time-symmetric laws of classical mechanics to the statistical reality of thermodynamics. Central to it is the concept of information, which acts as the bridge between the microscopic and the macroscopic. I illustrate the theory with a concrete calculation of the entropy of argon at 4 K and 300 K, and end with a comparison between the teaching of the Sussex school and Danish teaching of heat — a field I know well after further training as a chartered engineer at DTH and a long career at both HTX and STX upper-secondary schools.

I The classical paradox

Symmetry & conservation

Newton, Lagrange and Hamilton — time without direction

The foundation of classical physics was laid by Isaac Newton and later reformulated in more abstract frameworks by Joseph-Louis Lagrange and William Rowan Hamilton. A common feature is time symmetry.

Hamilton's equations, which describe the evolution of a system through its energy, are invariant under the transformation t → −t. If you film two argon atoms colliding and play the film backwards, you cannot tell the difference — the motion still obeys all the laws of physics. In the world of mechanics past and future are equal; there is no arrow of time.

Liouville's theorem — the constancy of information

In 1838 Joseph Liouville proved a theorem on differential equations which Gibbs later applied to statistical ensembles, and which was to prove deeply problematic for thermodynamics: the density of states in phase space (the 6N-dimensional space of the positions and momenta of all particles) behaves like an incompressible fluid.

dρ dt = 0 Liouville · 1838

Phase-space volume is conserved. If we start with precise knowledge of a system — a small volume in phase space — this amount of "knowledge" will never disappear. The system can be stretched and twisted, but the amount of information is constant.

If information (low entropy) is conserved microscopically, how can entropy then increase macroscopically?

II The birth of thermodynamics

The language of irreversibility

While the theorists discussed symmetry, the engineers worked with the machines of reality.

Émile Clapeyron
1834

Revived Sadi Carnot's work and formalised the (P, V) diagram. He showed how phase transitions are discontinuous jumps in material properties.

Rudolf Clausius
1865

Realised that heat always flows from hot to cold, and defined a new state function — entropy — to quantify irreversibility.

dS T ≥ dQ Clausius

With Clausius the universe got a direction. The arrow of time points towards higher entropy — towards what he called "heat death".

III The statistical solution

Boltzmann, Shannon & the demon

The conflict between Liouville's conservation and Clausius' growth was resolved by introducing statistics and information. Ludwig Boltzmann linked entropy directly to probability — we never know the precise microstate, only the macrostate:

S = kB ln W Boltzmann

W is the number of possible microstates. Entropy is not a fundamental force, but a statistical probability: a system tends towards equilibrium simply because there are far more ways of being in equilibrium than of being ordered.

Maxwell's demon and its exorcism

James Clerk Maxwell challenged the second law with his "demon", which sorts molecules without doing work.

The demon sorts fast molecules from slow ones — apparently without work. But where does the information come from?

The paradox was resolved in several stages. In 1929 Leo Szilard pointed out that the demon must acquire information about the molecules — and this measurement must be associated with an increase in entropy. In the 1950s Léon Brillouin extended this: the demon needs photons (light) to see the molecules. The light has an energy cost that increases the entropy of the surroundings more than the demon lowers the entropy of the system.

In 1948 Claude Shannon formalised information mathematically. He defined information as a reduction of uncertainty:

H = −∑ p ln p Shannon · 1948

— identical, apart from a constant, with Boltzmann's entropy. It was von Neumann who advised Shannon to call the quantity entropy.

Landauer's principle — erasure costs

In 1961 Rolf Landauer (IBM) turned the understanding upside down, and Charles Bennett (IBM) completed the argument in 1982: the measurement process itself can in principle be carried out reversibly, if you copy data. The problem arises when the demon's memory becomes full: to continue, it must erase old data — an irreversible logical operation in which two states become one. According to Landauer's principle, erasing 1 bit increases the entropy of the surroundings by at least kB ln 2, i.e. it releases an amount of heat of at least:

Q = kBT ln 2 Landauer · 1961

It is forgetting, not learning, that costs energy and increases the entropy of the universe.

Landauer's insight

Heisenberg pixelates phase space

When trying to calculate the absolute entropy, classical physics ran into the fact that phase space is continuous — infinitely many states. We taught students that one could only calculate a change in entropy, not an absolute entropy. That is actually not true. The solution came with quantum physics: as early as 1912 Sackur and Tetrode divided phase space into cells of size h³, and Heisenberg's uncertainty relation (1927) gave the explanation:

Δx · Δp ∼ h Heisenberg

Phase space is "pixelated" into cells of size h³, where h is Planck's constant. This makes the number of states W finite — and absolute entropy possible to calculate.

Sidebar · Gibbs' paradox

When two gases are not two gases

Two containers with a partition. In the first: oxygen on one side, nitrogen on the other. If the partition is removed, the gases mix — we have less information about where the molecules are, and the entropy increases. In the second: only argon on both sides. If the partition is removed, formally the same thing happens — but no work is done, and the entropy does not increase. Since the gas molecules are identical, we cannot tell them apart and have therefore not lost any information. Quantum physics resolves this formally: in Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac statistics identical particles are fundamentally indistinguishable.

V An everyday example

The bookseller's memory

Think of the old bookshops where the books stood higgledy-piggledy. To the customer the shop looks like a huge mess — he knows only the macrostate (a pile of books), and the number of possible ways the books can lie (W) is enormous. For him the entropy is high. For the bookseller, who knows exactly where each book is, W = 1, and S = kB ln(1) = 0.

If the bookseller forgets it all, the information in his brain is erased — exactly as in Landauer's principle. The system goes from "known microstate" to "unknown microstate", and the entropy increases. Even more interesting: if someone comes by and tidies up alphabetically, they have carried out an irreversible erasure of the bookseller's unique correlation between location and book title. The new state looks ordered, but the process required erasing information — and the heat from the person who hauled the books around increased the entropy of the surroundings.

Entropy is not mess. It is loss of data.

VI The Sussex school

Finn & Leggett

The teaching approach at the University of Sussex in the 70s and 80s was unique in Britain, shaped by The Sussex Experiment — an interdisciplinary structure that broke down barriers between subjects. I had to take a subject from "the arts" and chose archaeology of Crete.

Personal tutor

Colin Finn

Thermal Physics (1986)

The epitome of Sussex teaching: rigorous, clear and straight to the point. He insisted that students understood the difference between reversible and irreversible processes — not just as mathematics, but as physical reality. His teaching of availability and entropy as "degradation of energy quality" gave an intuitive understanding that many standard textbooks lacked.

Tutor in particle physics

Sir Tony Leggett

Nobel Prize 2003 · superfluid ³He

It was Leggett who offered me a place at Sussex. While Finn took care of the foundations, Leggett challenged the students with the deepest paradoxes of physics: the conflict between quantum mechanics (reversible) and the macroscopic world (irreversible). A heretical thought for many — typical of Sussex's open environment.

Leggett taught students to ask the uncomfortable question: Is entropy a fundamental law, or merely a consequence of our inability to calculate — our coarse-graining?

VII Perspective

Sussex vs. Danish teaching of heat

Brighton · UK

The Sussex tradition

Great emphasis on philosophical consequences and an early link to information theory. Conceptual clarity before formalism. Discussions of the arrow of time and the limits of quantum mechanics were respectable — even for first-years.

DK · STX / DTU

The Danish approach

Upper-secondary school introduces heat phenomenologically: the equation of state and the first law. Entropy is often treated qualitatively as "disorder". The statistical link (S = k ln W) is rarely on the syllabus at B level. At university the Landau/Lifshitz tradition is strong — mathematically heavy from the start, ensemble-focused.

However, the link to information theory (Shannon) has become more prominent in recent times — inspired by exactly the kind of thinking that Sussex pioneered 50–60 years ago. I would like to see it also inspire physics teaching here in Denmark at upper-secondary level.

The journey from Newton's reversible mechanics to today's information physics shows that entropy is far more than "mess". It is the price of ignorance in a quantum-mechanical universe — and when we say that entropy increases, we are really saying that the information about the system's microstate disappears into Heisenberg's shadows.

CHAPTER 1

Point and line

Imagine a point, a tiny little point. No matter how close you get to it, or how far you zoom in, it stays a tiny little point.

When you draw a point on paper, what you actually make is a small round dot. If you look more closely at it or zoom in, it will look more and more like a round blob on the paper.

That is not how it is with a real point. The closest we can come to seeing a point is actually a star far out in space. Although many are suns just like our own Sun, they are so far away that until recently they still looked like a point in our most powerful telescopes. We could not see any of their round shape at all. And on top of that there were many very bright streaks, lines, radiating out from the point where the star was. Of course, the radiating streaks were something the telescope created.

But let us dwell on that picture for a moment.

Now we can imagine a point, and from that point many shining streaks radiate out — lines, in fact.

Look, we have already got hold of the simplest parts of geometry: the point and the line.

But now let us draw this pattern on paper, sticking at first to the geometry we can have on a flat piece of paper on our desk or kitchen table.

TRY IT YOURSELF

OK, now you should have a blank sheet of white A4 paper in front of you. You could fetch it from the printer, in the tray where the paper is kept.

With your pencil, draw a dot or a point in the middle of the sheet. It does not look like much, but you have drawn the simplest geometric part there is. It cannot get simpler than a point.

So what? you may think. Do you remember what you read, and the streaks and lines that radiated from a star? They went in all directions from the same point. We say that they all met there at the point.

TRY IT YOURSELF

Now try drawing straight strokes yourself — that is, lines — that all pass through the point. Use a ruler so they look neat and correct.

How many can you draw? Could you keep on and on drawing such straight lines?

Yes, you actually could, and you would never be finished.

But now all the many possibilities suddenly become one. Now draw a second point on one of the straight lines. How many straight lines can now pass through both points, that is, from one point to the other? Only one can.

What have you discovered now?

You have now discovered that a line can be determined by two points.

So now we have really got further — from one point to two points and the line that connects them.

If I had said that you did not need to use straight lines and your ruler, you could keep on finding new paths between the two points. But if we stick to straight lines, there is only one — and do you know what? That line has to be the shortest path.

Why is that? Just think about it. As a hint I will ask you: What if the straight line were not the shortest? What would that mean?

It would mean that there was a shorter route. But wait a moment — is that possible? No. Because we have already found out that there is only one straight line from one point to the other. All the other paths between the two points are bent — they are curves — and of those there are infinitely many. But they are all longer than the straight line. That is why the straight line is the shortest.

Let us take a break and lean back for a moment.

SUMMARY — what have we discovered?
  • A point. That a point is the simplest geometric part.
  • Two points. That two points determine a line.
  • A straight line. That a line is the shortest distance or route between two points.

If we lifted our paper up from the desk and put it on a wall, these three statements would still be true. They do not only hold in two dimensions.

OK, we have now got to two points and a straight line that passes through both of them and connects them.

What more could we add? A new line or a new point — and where?

Let us draw a new line segment with our ruler and pencil. Perhaps you should start with a fresh, clean piece of paper. First draw two points and then a straight line connecting them.

Where should I put the new line? Through one of the points? Across the line that is already there? Or beside it, so that it neither passes through one of the points nor crosses the line that is already there?

What if we do all three things and then see what we get, and what it tells us?

I hope you are now beginning to see where this is going? What do you think?

CHAPTER 2

Angles and triangles

Now let us draw an extra straight line through one of the points. Notice what happens: the two lines now form an angle. That is a completely new concept.

DEFINITION

An angle is a measure of how much you have to turn one line before it coincides with the other. Think of it as a turn about the point.

Just as a line segment has a length that can be measured in centimetres — look at your ruler — an angle has a size that is measured in degrees.

If you turned a line segment all the way round the point, so that it ended where it began, we say that it has turned 360 degrees. We will come back to that number later, when we talk about the calendar and about measuring time.

So now we have defined what an angle is. The new line could lie across, it could lie at a slant, or it could be parallel to the first.

If we lay the lines parallel, they never meet, no matter how far we extend them. But if they cross each other, an angle is always formed.

Now let us place yet another line. See what happens: all three lines cross each other, and inside the space they enclose we have a triangle — another geometric part.

A triangle has three corners, and in each corner there is an angle. Let us call them A, B and C.

TRY IT YOURSELF

Now comes something strange and lovely. Try drawing several different triangles — big, small, pointed and flat — and measure the three angles in each of them. Add them up. What do you get?

You get 180 degrees. Every single time. No matter what the triangle looks like, the three angles together always make 180 degrees.

REMEMBER

A + B + C = 180°

The three angles of a triangle always add up to 180 degrees.

Why do you think it always comes to 180? Try a little trick: cut the three corners off your triangle and lay them next to each other with their tips at the same point. They form a straight line — and a straight line is half a turn, that is, 180 degrees.

A proof

The cutting trick shows it nicely. But in mathematics we want to be completely sure and prove that it holds for every triangle. Here is the proof — it uses a parallel line.

Look at the triangle with corners A, B and C. Angle A sits at corner A, angle C at corner C, and angle B up at B.

Now take a copy of angles A and C and slide them up along the sides until they meet angle B up at B. The three angles now lie neatly next to each other.

Notice the outermost part: the two outer arms together form one long, straight line through B — and that line is parallel to side AC.

What else do you see? The three angles A, B and C now go from one side of the line to the other. Together they fill half a turn round B.

And half a turn is half of 360 degrees — that is, 180 degrees. So A + B + C = 180 degrees. In every triangle, every single time.

Look, now we have proved something important in geometry. Well done!

Exercises

Let us try it out. Take paper and pencil — and remember all the time that the three angles of a triangle always add up to 180 degrees.

  1. How many different straight lines can you draw through a single point? And how many through two points?
  2. Two ants have to go from point A to point B. One walks in a straight line, the other in an arc. Which one takes the shortest path — and why?
  3. How many degrees does a clock hand turn if it goes half a turn round? And if it goes a quarter turn?
  4. An angle of 90 degrees is called a right angle. Find three places in your room where two lines meet at a right angle.
  5. In a triangle two of the angles are 60 degrees and 70 degrees. How big is the third angle?
  6. A triangle has an angle of 90 degrees and one of 45 degrees. How big is the last angle? And what kind of triangle is it?
  7. In an equilateral triangle all three angles are equal. How many degrees is each angle then?
  8. In a triangle one angle is 80 degrees, and the other two are equal. How big is each of them?
  9. Can a triangle have two sides that are parallel? Explain why or why not.
  10. Challenge: Can a triangle have two right angles (two angles of 90 degrees)? Use what we have just proved to explain the answer.

Answers

(only look once you have tried yourself)

  1. Through one point: infinitely many. Through two points: only one.
  2. The one walking in a straight line — because a straight line is the shortest path between two points.
  3. Half a turn is 180 degrees. A quarter turn is 90 degrees.
  4. Your own answers — for example the corner of a table, a door, a window or a book against a shelf.
  5. 180 − 60 − 70 = 50 degrees.
  6. 180 − 90 − 45 = 45 degrees. It is a right-angled triangle (it has a right angle) — and at the same time isosceles, because two angles are equal.
  7. 180 ÷ 3 = 60 degrees in each angle.
  8. The two angles together make 180 − 80 = 100 degrees, so each of them is 50 degrees.
  9. No. The three sides of a triangle meet in pairs at the corners, but parallel lines never meet. So none of the sides can be parallel.
  10. No. Two right angles are already 90 + 90 = 180 degrees, and then there are 0 degrees left for the third angle. But a triangle must have three angles that together make 180. So it is impossible.

CHAPTER 3

Similar triangles

Before we move on to the quadrilateral, we are going to use the triangle for something really useful. Listen.

DEFINITION

Two triangles can have exactly the same shape but different sizes — like a small and a large picture of the same thing. We say that they are similar. That means they have the same angles.

And when two triangles have the same shape, all their sides are in the same ratio. If the large triangle is twice as big, every single side is twice as long. That number — how many times bigger one is than the other — we call the scale factor.

If we know all the lengths and angles of one triangle, we do not need to measure very much on the other to know it completely. If we know two of its angles, we know it has the same shape — the third angle follows from the 180 degrees — and then we only need one length to find the scale factor. If instead we know two lengths and the angle between them, we can also work out the rest.

Once we have the scale factor, we can calculate all the sides we could not measure ourselves. And that is a fantastic idea — because now we can measure things that are far too tall to reach.

How tall is the tree?

Imagine a tall tree. You cannot reach the top with a tape measure. But the tree casts a shadow on the ground — and that you can measure.

TRY IT YOURSELF

Now stand a stick whose height you know upright beside it. It casts a shadow too. The Sun's rays strike the tree and the stick at exactly the same angle, so the two triangles — tree-with-shadow and stick-with-shadow — are similar.

Then the tree's height compared with the tree's shadow is the same as the stick's height compared with the stick's shadow. In other words:

REMEMBER

height of tree = height of stick × (tree's shadow ÷ stick's shadow)

Measure the two shadows and the stick's height, put in the numbers — and you have the tree's height without climbing a single metre.

A worked example

Let us try with a flagpole. A stick of 2 metres casts a shadow of 3 metres. At the same time the flagpole casts a shadow of 12 metres. How tall is the flagpole?

EXAMPLE

First we find the ratio of the shadows: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. So the flagpole's shadow is 4 times as long as the stick's. Then the flagpole must also be 4 times as tall as the stick: 2 metres × 4 = 8 metres. The flagpole is 8 metres tall.

The same idea works on a chimney, a tower or any tree at all. You just need a shadow, a stick and a little geometry.

Exercises

Remember all the time: similar triangles have the same shape, and their sides are in the same ratio — the scale factor.

  1. Two similar triangles: the small one has sides of 3, 4 and 5 cm. The large one is twice as big. How long are its sides?
  2. A stick of 1 metre casts a shadow of 2 metres. At the same time a tree casts a shadow of 10 metres. How tall is the tree?
  3. Two triangles are similar. A side of the small one is 6 cm, and the corresponding side of the large one is 18 cm. What is the scale factor?
  4. A person of 1.5 metres casts a shadow of 1 metre. At the same time a chimney casts a shadow of 8 metres. How tall is the chimney?
  5. Two similar triangles. The small one has sides of 4 cm and 6 cm. The large triangle's shortest side is 12 cm. How long is its other side?
  6. Two similar triangles have scale factor 3. The small triangle's sides are 2, 3 and 4 cm. How long are the large one's sides?
  7. Challenge: You want to measure a flagpole, but there is neither sun nor shadow. Can you think of another way of making two similar triangles?

Answers

(only look once you have tried yourself)

  1. 6, 8 and 10 cm (all multiplied by 2).
  2. The ratio is 10 ÷ 2 = 5, so the tree is 1 m × 5 = 5 metres tall.
  3. 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
  4. The ratio is 8 ÷ 1 = 8, so the chimney is 1.5 m × 8 = 12 metres tall.
  5. The ratio is 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so the other side is 6 cm × 3 = 18 cm.
  6. 6, 9 and 12 cm (all multiplied by 3).
  7. Your own answers — for example: put a mirror on the ground between you and the flagpole, and walk back until you see the top in the mirror. Then your eyes, the mirror and the flagpole form two similar triangles. Or hold a stick upright at arm's length and use the ratio between the stick and your arm.

CHAPTER 4

Pythagoras' theorem

We will stay with the triangle a little longer, because it hides one of the most famous discoveries in all of mathematics. It is more than 2500 years old and bears the name of the Greek thinker Pythagoras.

The theorem is about a very special kind of triangle: the right-angled one. That is a triangle with one right angle — a corner of 90 degrees, like the corner of a piece of paper.

DEFINITION

In a right-angled triangle we call the two short sides (the ones at the right angle) the legs, and the long, slanting side opposite the right angle the hypotenuse. Here we call the two short sides a and b — and the hypotenuse c.

Pythagoras discovered something astonishing: if you draw a square on each of the three sides, the two small squares together are just as big as the large one.

And here is the beautiful part — he did not need numbers or algebra at all to show it. Algebra as we know it today only came many hundreds of years later. Pythagoras could make do with moving some congruent right-angled triangles around. Congruent figures have the same shape, the same angles and the same size. They can be laid exactly on top of each other and match perfectly, with nothing sticking out or missing.

Look at the large square. It is the square on c — you can see that because all four sides have length c. Inside it lie four identical triangles, spun round like a windmill, with a small square left over in the middle.

Now take the same four triangles and the small square, and rearrange them. Then they form exactly two new squares: the square on a and the square on b. We have not removed or added anything — just moved the pieces around.

Since the pieces take up the same space in both places, the square on c must be just as big as the squares on a and b together.

REMEMBER

a² + b² = c²

In a right-angled triangle the square on the long side equals the other two squares together.

That is the whole proof — without a single calculation. Only many hundreds of years later was it written with letters, as we do now.

EXAMPLE

A right-angled triangle has legs of 3 cm and 4 cm. Then a² + b² = 3×3 + 4×4 = 9 + 16 = 25. And because 25 = 5×5, the hypotenuse is c = 5 cm. This is the famous 3-4-5 triangle, which craftsmen have used for thousands of years to make perfectly accurate right angles.

Exercises

Remember: in a right-angled triangle a² + b² = c², where a and b are the two short sides and c is the long one (the hypotenuse).

  1. What is the name of the special kind of triangle that Pythagoras' theorem is about?
  2. What do we call the long, slanting side of a right-angled triangle?
  3. A right-angled triangle has legs of 6 cm and 8 cm. What is a² + b²? And how long is the hypotenuse?
  4. Did Pythagoras use algebra (calculating with letters) for his proof? Explain briefly.
  5. A right-angled triangle has legs of 5 cm and 12 cm. How long is the hypotenuse? (Tip: 25 + 144 = 169, and 169 = 13×13.)
  6. Challenge: A ladder leans against a wall. Its foot stands 3 metres from the wall, and the ladder touches the wall 4 metres up. How long is the ladder?

Answers

(only look once you have tried yourself)

  1. The right-angled triangle — a triangle with a right angle (90 degrees).
  2. The hypotenuse.
  3. a² + b² = 36 + 64 = 100, and because 100 = 10×10, the hypotenuse is 10 cm.
  4. No. He moved four identical triangles around and compared areas. Algebra only came many hundreds of years later.
  5. 25 + 144 = 169 = 13×13, so the hypotenuse is 13 cm.
  6. The ladder is the hypotenuse: 3×3 + 4×4 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5×5, so the ladder is 5 metres long.

CHAPTER 5

The quadrilateral

Do you remember that we have kept promising each other the quadrilateral? Now we are there. Get your paper out again.

We already have two points and a line. Now put a third point, so that we get a triangle — and then a fourth point. Connect the four points with four lines so that they enclose a space. Now you have a quadrilateral: four corners and four sides.

So a quadrilateral is simply the next step after the triangle. Where the triangle had three corners, the quadrilateral has four.

How many degrees now?

We found out that the three angles of a triangle always make 180 degrees. But what about the four angles of a quadrilateral? Try to guess before you read on.

Here is a beautiful trick. Draw a line from one corner across to the opposite corner. Such a line we call a diagonal. What do you see now?

The diagonal divides the quadrilateral into two triangles! And we already know that each triangle has angles that together make 180 degrees.

Two triangles, each with 180 degrees, together make 180 + 180 = 360 degrees. So the four angles of a quadrilateral always make 360 degrees.

REMEMBER

4 angles = 360°

A quadrilateral can be divided into two triangles by a diagonal: 180° + 180° = 360°.

Notice the number: 360 degrees is exactly one full turn — the same number we met when we turned a line all the way round a point. Now it turns up again.

Many kinds of quadrilateral

Not all quadrilaterals look the same. There is a whole family of them, and some have been given their own names.

The square has four equal sides and four right angles — it is the most regular of them all. The rectangle also has four right angles, but two of the sides are longer than the others.

The parallelogram is skewed, but its opposite sides are parallel in pairs — do you remember the parallel lines from before? The rhombus is a parallelogram in which all four sides are equal. And the trapezium has just one pair of parallel sides.

But whatever kind of quadrilateral you choose, the four angles always make 360 degrees. Try it yourself with the square: four right angles, 90 + 90 + 90 + 90 = 360. It works!

Exercises

Remember: the four angles of a quadrilateral always make 360 degrees — and a quadrilateral can always be divided into two triangles by a diagonal.

  1. How many corners and how many sides does a quadrilateral have?
  2. In a quadrilateral three of the angles are 90, 90 and 100 degrees. How big is the fourth angle?
  3. How many degrees is each angle in a square? And what do the four make together?
  4. You draw a diagonal in a quadrilateral. How many triangles does it divide the quadrilateral into?
  5. A quadrilateral has the angles 80, 100 and 80 degrees. What is the fourth angle?
  6. Which kind of quadrilateral has four equal sides and four right angles? And which has only one pair of parallel sides?
  7. Challenge: A pentagon (five corners) can be divided into triangles by diagonals from one corner. How many triangles does that make, and how many degrees do the five angles make together?

Answers

(only look once you have tried yourself)

  1. Four corners and four sides.
  2. 360 − 90 − 90 − 100 = 80 degrees.
  3. Each angle is 90 degrees, and together 4 × 90 = 360 degrees.
  4. Into two triangles.
  5. 360 − 80 − 100 − 80 = 100 degrees.
  6. A square has four equal sides and four right angles. A quadrilateral with only one pair of parallel sides is a trapezium.
  7. The diagonals from one corner divide the pentagon into 3 triangles, so the five angles together make 3 × 180 = 540 degrees.

CHAPTER 6

The circle

We ended the last section with a question: what happens if we keep adding corners to a figure?

Let us try. A triangle has three corners, a quadrilateral four. If we draw a pentagon, a hexagon, a decagon … the edges get shorter and shorter, and the figure gets rounder and rounder. In the end, when there are infinitely many tiny corners, we cannot see them at all any more. Then we have a circle.

That is how it all fits together: the circle is in a way a polygon with infinitely many sides — just as the very first point we started with had infinitely many lines.

The parts of the circle

DEFINITION

A circle is something very special: it is all the points that lie exactly the same distance from one midpoint. That midpoint we call the centre.

The distance from the centre out to the circle is called the radius. If we draw a line all the way through the centre, from one side to the other, we get the diameter — and it is always twice as long as the radius. The round curve itself, all the way round, is called the circumference or perimeter.

All the way round

Do you remember that a full turn was 360 degrees? That holds for the circle too. If you go all the way round the circumference and are back where you started, you have been 360 degrees round.

Halfway round is 180 degrees, a quarter of the way is 90 degrees — exactly as when we turned a line about a point. The circle and the angle are two sides of the same coin.

How far is it round? The number π

Here comes one of the most beautiful things in all of mathematics. Take a circle and roll it out into a straight line. How long will it be?

The answer is always the same: the circumference is a little more than three times as long as the diameter. To be precise it is 3.14 times as long — and that number has got its own name: the Greek letter π, pronounced pi.

REMEMBER

Circumference = π × diameter

π ≈ 3.14 — and it holds for every single circle, big or small.

So if you know the diameter, you can always find the circumference: circumference = π × diameter. The strange and lovely thing is that it holds for every single circle in the whole world, big or small.

There is also a rule for the area inside the circle — area = π × radius × radius — but that is a story for another day.

The circle in history

The circle has also left its mark on history — in some quite unexpected ways.

There is a famous story about the Italian painter Giotto. The Pope's envoy came to find out whether Giotto was skilled enough for a great commission, and asked for a sample of his skill. Giotto dipped his brush, put his other hand on his hip and painted — in a single stroke and completely freehand — a perfect circle. The envoy was speechless. (The story was written down many years later, so it may be a little too good to be entirely true. But it says something about how difficult and how beautiful a perfectly round brushstroke really is.)

King Arthur's Round Table is also thanks to the circle. A round table has neither a grand head nor a lowly foot, so no knight came to sit more grandly than the others — all were equal. In this way the perfect symmetry of the circle became an image of equality between people.

And then something to think about. The Incas of South America built fantastic roads and mighty stone walls, but they never invented the wheel to ride on — nor the arch in architecture, the true stone arch. Two of the most ingenious round ideas in all of history, and a great and skilful culture managed completely without them. Perhaps because their mountains were so steep that wheels were not much use, and because their walls stood strong even without arches.

Exercises

Remember: the diameter is twice as long as the radius, a full trip round is 360 degrees, and circumference = π × diameter (with π ≈ 3.14).

  1. A circle has a radius of 5 cm. How long is the diameter?
  2. How many degrees are there all the way round a circle? And halfway round?
  3. A circle has a diameter of 10 cm. How long is the circumference? (use π ≈ 3.14)
  4. What do we call the distance from the centre out to the circle?
  5. A plate has a diameter of 20 cm. How far is it round the edge? (π ≈ 3.14)
  6. Why can you say that a circle is like a polygon with a great many corners?
  7. Challenge: A circle has a radius of 3 cm. First find the diameter, and then use π to find the circumference.

Answers

(only look once you have tried yourself)

  1. The diameter is 2 × 5 = 10 cm.
  2. All the way round is 360 degrees, and halfway round is 180 degrees.
  3. Circumference = 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm.
  4. We call it the radius.
  5. Circumference = 3.14 × 20 = 62.8 cm.
  6. Because the figure gets rounder and rounder the more corners it gets. A circle is like a polygon with infinitely many tiny sides.
  7. The diameter is 2 × 3 = 6 cm, and the circumference is 3.14 × 6 = 18.84 cm.

But the circle can do even more. If we take a very special circle — one with radius exactly 1 — it opens the door to one of the most useful things in all of mathematics: calculating triangles from their angles. Let us look at it.

CHAPTER 7

Sine, cosine and tangent

Remember the circle from before. Now we are going to use a very special one: a circle with radius exactly 1. We call it the unit circle — and it is one of the most useful drawings in all of mathematics.

Draw the unit circle with its centre where a horizontal and a vertical axis cross each other. Put a point P out on the circle, and draw a radius out to it. The radius forms an angle with the horizontal axis — we call that angle v.

Cosine and sine

DEFINITION

The point P on the unit circle has two coordinates: how far along it is in the horizontal direction, and how far up it is in the vertical direction. The horizontal distance we call the cosine of v — written cos v. The vertical one we call the sine of v — written sin v. In short: P = (cos v, sin v).

Let us see what that means. When the angle v is small, P lies almost all the way out to the right: then cos v is large (close to 1), and sin v small (close to 0). As v grows, and P climbs upwards along the circle, sin v gets bigger and cos v smaller. So sine and cosine tell us where on the circle the point is.

Tangent

There is a third quantity as well. If we extend the radius as a straight line until it hits a vertical line that just touches the circle out at x = 1, we read off the tangent of v — written tan v.

REMEMBER

tan v = sin v / cos v

The tangent is the sine divided by the cosine — it tells how steeply the radius slopes.

The steeper the radius, the bigger tan v becomes. If the radius lies completely horizontal (v = 0), tan v = 0. As the radius rises towards vertical, tan v grows very fast.

From circle to triangle

Now look really carefully at the unit circle. Inside it a right-angled triangle is hiding! It has the horizontal side cos v, the vertical side sin v, and the radius as hypotenuse — and the radius is exactly 1.

Here comes the beautiful part, and it builds on something we already know: similar triangles. If we make the unit circle's little triangle twice as big, all the sides become twice as long — but the angles are the same. And because the angle v does not change, the ratio between the sides is exactly the same.

DEFINITION

In any right-angled triangle with the angle v we name the sides after v: the side directly opposite v is called the opposite, the side next to v (between v and the right angle) is called the adjacent, and the long slanting side is the hypotenuse.

REMEMBER

sin v = opposite / hypotenuse
cos v = adjacent / hypotenuse
tan v = opposite / adjacent

v is the angle in a right-angled triangle.

That means something important: if you know the angle and just one side of a right-angled triangle, you can find the others. That is exactly what surveyors, carpenters and astronomers use all the time.

A worked example

A ladder leans against a wall. The ladder is 4 metres long — that is the hypotenuse — and it makes an angle of 60° with the ground. How high up the wall does the ladder reach?

EXAMPLE

The height is the side opposite the 60°, so we use sine: sin 60° = height ÷ 4. On a calculator sin 60° ≈ 0.87. So the height is ≈ 0.87 × 4 ≈ 3.5 metres.

If instead we want to know how far the foot of the ladder is from the wall, we use cosine: cos 60° = distance ÷ 4, and cos 60° = 0.5, so the distance is 0.5 × 4 = 2 metres.

Exercises

Remember: on the unit circle P = (cos v, sin v). In a right-angled triangle sin v = opposite ÷ hypotenuse, cos v = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse, and tan v = opposite ÷ adjacent.

  2. A point on the unit circle has the coordinates (cos v, sin v). What is the point's horizontal distance from the centre equal to?
  3. Explain in your own words what the adjacent, the opposite and the hypotenuse are in a right-angled triangle with the angle v.
  4. A right-angled triangle has the angle v, an opposite side of 3 and a hypotenuse of 5. What is sin v?
  5. Write tan v using sin v and cos v.
  6. A 6-metre ladder leans against a wall and makes an angle of 60° with the ground. How high up does it reach? (sin 60° ≈ 0.87)

Answers

(only look once you have tried yourself)

  1. The radius is 1.
  2. It is equal to cos v.
  3. The hypotenuse is the long slanting side. The opposite side lies directly opposite the angle v. The adjacent side lies next to v — between v and the right angle.
  4. sin v = opposite ÷ hypotenuse = 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6.
  5. tan v = sin v ÷ cos v.
  6. Height = sin 60° × 6 ≈ 0.87 × 6 ≈ 5.2 metres.
  7. tan v = opposite ÷ adjacent = 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75. Hypotenuse: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5², so the hypotenuse is 5 — it is the 3-4-5 triangle again!

The circle has given us a lot: the circumference, the number π — and now sine, cosine and tangent, which open up the whole world of the triangle. But the circle hides one more thing.

CHAPTER 8

Ellipse, parabola and hyperbola

The circle was something special, but it has a whole family of relatives. You meet them whenever you cut through a cone in different ways — that is why they are called conic sections.

Imagine a cone, like the tip of an ice-cream cone or a Christmas tree. If you cut straight across, you get a circle. But if you tilt the knife a little, something new happens.

The cuts have been given names: circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola. We already know the circle — now we take the other three.

The ellipse

DEFINITION

An ellipse is a circle that has been squashed a little flat — like an egg or an oval plate seen at a slant. But it has a beautiful secret: instead of one centre it has two special points inside it. We call them foci.

TRY IT YOURSELF

Here is a trick you can try yourself. Stick two drawing pins into a piece of cardboard, and tie a loose string between them. Now put the tip of a pencil in the string, pull it taut, and move the pencil all the way round. You draw a perfect ellipse!

Why does it work? Because the string has the same length all the way round. So wherever the pencil is, the distance from one focus, over to the pencil and back to the other focus, is always the same. That is the very definition of an ellipse.

The planets — and our own Earth — by the way run round the Sun in ellipses, with the Sun at one focus. Johannes Kepler discovered that more than 400 years ago.

The parabola

If you tilt the knife so that the cut becomes parallel to the side of the cone, the curve does not close. It just carries on to infinity and opens out like a bowl. That is a parabola.

DEFINITION

The parabola has only one focus — and a line we call the directrix. The special thing is that every single point on the parabola lies the same distance from the focus and from the directrix.

If you throw a ball, it follows a parabola on its way up and down again. So does a jet of water from a hose. And because a parabola collects all light and all signals at its focus, a satellite dish has exactly this shape.

The hyperbola

If you cut very steeply — so steeply that the knife hits both halves of a double cone — you get two separate, mirror-image curves. That is a hyperbola.

DEFINITION

The hyperbola, like the ellipse, has two foci. But here it is not the sum that stays the same: it is the difference between the two distances that is always the same.

Hyperbolas also turn up in real life — for example in the elegant, waisted shape of a large cooling tower.

The three shapes around us

Once you know what to look for, you see the conic sections everywhere.

An ellipse in a planet's orbit, a parabola in a ball's throw or a jet of water, a hyperbola in a cooling tower. And it all began with a cone and a knife.

But geometry has one more place it wants to take us — right back to what gave it its name more than two thousand years ago.

CHAPTER 9

Geometry — measuring the Earth

Finally we shall see where the word geometry itself comes from — and why it is perhaps the most beautiful name in all of mathematics. The word is Greek. 'Geo' means the Earth, and 'metron' means to measure. So geometry quite simply means: measuring the Earth.

And that is exactly what people have used it for. With nothing but angles, shadows and common sense they have measured the Earth itself — long before anyone could travel all the way round it.

Eratosthenes measures the Earth

More than two thousand years ago there lived in Egypt a man named Eratosthenes. He had heard of something strange: in the town of Syene, at midday on the longest day of summer, you could see the Sun reflected right at the bottom of a deep well. So the Sun stood exactly at the zenith — directly overhead — and a stick there would cast no shadow at all.

On the same day and at the same time Eratosthenes set a stick upright in Alexandria, a good way further north. And there the stick cast a clear shadow. Why? Because the Earth is curved! The Sun's rays arrive almost exactly parallel, but the two towns face the Sun differently — like two sticks on a large ball.

Now came the clever part. Eratosthenes measured how big the angle A of the shadow in Alexandria was. It was about 7 degrees. And here is the beautiful thing: that angle is exactly the same as the angle between the two towns seen from deep inside the centre of the Earth. (Do you remember the alternate angles from the chapter on triangles? It is them again.)

REMEMBER

7° is roughly 1/50 of 360°

So the distance between the towns is 1/50 of the Earth's whole circumference. Eratosthenes knew the distance — and could multiply up.

His answer was incredibly close to what we know today to be right: about 40,000 kilometres all the way round the Earth. Just think — the circumference of the whole globe, found with a stick and a shadow.

They knew the Earth is round

Many people believe that people in the old days went around thinking the Earth was flat. But the learned Greeks knew perfectly well that it is a sphere — and they had good reasons for it.

During a lunar eclipse the Earth slides in between the Sun and the Moon, so that the Earth's own shadow falls across the Moon. That shadow always has a curved edge — an arc of a circle. And only one single shape always casts a round shadow, no matter which way it faces: a sphere. A plate would now and then cast a flat, elongated shadow — but that never happens. So the Earth must be round.

And there was more. When a ship sails away from us out over the sea, it does not just disappear by getting smaller and smaller until it becomes a tiny dot. It sinks from the bottom first: the hull disappears below the horizon while the mast can still be seen above. That is only possible if the surface of the sea curves away. On a flat earth the ship would just shrink.

Sundials — time in a shadow

Shadows can also tell us what time it is. A sundial is perhaps the very oldest measuring instrument we have — and it is pure geometry.

A slanting rod — it is called a gnomon — casts a shadow onto a dial. As the Sun moves across the sky during the day, the shadow moves with it, and you read the time from which way the shadow points.

The neat trick is that the gnomon must slope exactly as much as the latitude of the place. Then it points straight at the celestial pole — the point that the whole starry sky seems to turn around — and the shadow shows the right time all year round. Geometry and sky in one small instrument.

The place of shadows in science

When you think about it, shadows have played an astonishingly large part in how we have come to know the world.

A shadow from a stick measured the size of the whole Earth. The Earth's shadow on the Moon showed that it is a sphere. And during a solar eclipse it is the Moon's shadow that rushes across the Earth and plunges us into darkness in the middle of the day — just then astronomers have been able to measure things about the Sun that they could otherwise never see.

And strangest of all: even light has its own kind of shadow. If you send light through two thin slits side by side, you would expect two bright stripes to come out on the other side. But no — there are many. A whole pattern of bright and dark stripes appears.

That pattern reveals something deep. Light behaves like waves on water: where two wave crests meet, it becomes extra bright, and where a crest meets a trough, they cancel each other out and it becomes dark. This little game of light and shadow is one of the most important experiments in all of physics — and it began with looking very closely at how light and shadow behave.

  3. For example: the Earth's round shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse, and that ships disappear from the bottom first when they sail away over the horizon.

  5. That light behaves like waves, which can add together into bright stripes or cancel each other out into dark stripes.

Now see how far we have come. We started with a single tiny point. From it grew a line, an angle, a triangle, a quadrilateral, a circle, sine and cosine, the whole family of conic sections — and at the very end the Earth itself, measured with a shadow. All the way, the same simple ideas have faithfully followed us: points, lines, distances and angles. The most beautiful thing of all is that you can now draw it, measure it and understand why yourself. Geometry means measuring the Earth — but in reality it is a whole way of seeing the entire world. And now you can see it. Well done — and have great fun discovering even more.

CHAPTER 10

Bridges and domes — curves in architecture

We have met the straight line, the triangle, the circle and the conic sections on paper. But the same curves are also found out in the big world — they carry bridges over rivers, and they lift domes up towards the sky. Let us round off the booklet with a little tour and see where they turn up, where you least expect them.

The arch — the semicircle that carries its own weight

One of the oldest ways of building an opening in a wall is the arch. The Romans built it as a semicircle: a row of wedge-shaped stones pressing on each other all the way round, down to two solid piers. No stone can fall out, because it is clamped by its neighbours — the more weight the bridge carries, the harder the stones are pressed together.

That kind of arch can last for thousands of years — many Roman bridges and aqueducts still stand, more than 2000 years after they were built. But the semicircle is not the only shape an arch can have. Medieval master builders discovered that a more pointed arch — the pointed arch you see in many Gothic cathedrals — could carry even more and let the church rise even higher towards the sky.

The catenary — the secret of the suspension bridge

DEFINITION

If you hang a chain or a string loosely between two points, it forms a very particular curve all by itself. We call that curve a catenary. It looks like a parabola, but is actually a curve of its own — a little flatter at the bottom and steeper at the ends than the parabola.

TRY IT YOURSELF

Take a string or a thin chain. Hold it loosely at both ends, so that it hangs freely between your hands. Look at the shape — and then draw a parabola of the same depth beside it. They look very alike, but are not quite the same.

Master builders have used the catenary for centuries, long before anyone could calculate it — they could simply see that the shape held itself up. A hanging chain is in perfect equilibrium: every little piece of it is pulled only along the chain itself, never across it. If you turn the curve upside down, you get an arch that carries its own weight without bending or breaking at all. That is the secret behind many of the world's strongest arches and vaults.

In a suspension bridge it is not a chain but a thick steel cable that hangs between two tall pylons. From the cable vertical hangers run down to the roadway, and they carry the weight of the cars and trains up to the cable. Because the roadway spreads the weight evenly along the cable's horizontal extent — and not along the cable's own length — the curve of the main cable is actually a little closer to a pure parabola than to a catenary. But to the naked eye the two curves are almost impossible to tell apart.

The dome — an arch turned all the way round

Do you remember how the circle came about when we let a polygon have more and more corners? The dome builds on a similar idea — just in three dimensions. Take a semicircular arch, like the one we just saw in a bridge, and turn it all the way round, 360 degrees, about a vertical axis through its midpoint. Then you get a dome.

Just like the semicircular arch, the stones or bricks of the dome press together against each other, all the way round in every direction. That makes the dome incredibly strong — it can cover a huge space without a single column in the middle.

REMEMBER

The architect of St Paul's Cathedral, Christopher Wren, used the catenary when he designed the dome — the idea came from his friend Robert Hooke: hang up a chain, and turn the shape upside down. Between the inner dome, seen from below, and the outer one, seen from outside, there is a hidden, load-bearing brick cone, and its profile follows exactly the inverted hanging chain. It is this that carries the heavy lantern on top.

The onion dome — when the curve bulges out

Not all domes are hemispheres. If you travel to Russia, India or the Middle East, you meet onion domes: domes that bulge out wider than a hemisphere just before they narrow into a pointed top — exactly like the shape of an onion. The curve is neither a circle nor an ellipse, but a softly bent curve that first swings outwards and then inwards. It is called an ogee curve.

The onion dome has both a practical and an aesthetic advantage: the wide bulge leads rain and snow quickly away from the dome's surface, while the pointed top points boldly up towards the sky.

From chain to tent

Some of today's great roofs use the same idea as the hanging chain — just turned round and pulled taut instead of hanging loose. The O2 in London is held up by twelve tall masts and a net of steel cables that stretch a huge fabric between them. The fabric hangs in soft curves between the masts, like a string between two fingers — the same geometry that holds up a suspension bridge is used here to build a roof.

More domes around the world

Once you start looking for them, arcs and domes turn up everywhere you go.

CHAPTER 11

Conic sections in real life — from a thrown stone to a comet

In Chapter 8 we met the conic sections: the circle, the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola. They are not just beautiful figures on paper — they govern how a stone flies through the air, how a mirror can collect sunlight, and how planets and comets move through the Solar System. Let us look at some more examples.

The circle — the most efficient shape

Here is a surprising fact: of all flat shapes with the same perimeter, it is the circle that encloses the greatest area. If you have a rope of a certain length and have to fence in as large an area as possible, you should lay the rope in a circle — not in a square or any other shape.

REMEMBER

With the same perimeter the circle always wins. A square with perimeter 8 has an area of 4. A circle with the same perimeter 8 has an area of about 5.09 — almost a quarter more, without using more rope.

That is also why a soap bubble always becomes round: it automatically finds the shape that uses the least surface to contain a given amount of air.

The parabola — the path of a thrown stone

Throw a stone into the air without anything slowing it down — no air resistance — and its path becomes a pure parabola. That holds for any thrown object: a ball, a cannonball, a jet of water from a hose. Gravity pulls evenly downwards all the way, while the object moves forwards at constant speed, and that gives exactly the parabola shape we met in Chapter 8.

This idea is actually used to train astronauts. A special aircraft flies up in a large arc and then follows exactly the same parabolic path that a freely thrown object would follow. For a short time — about 20–25 seconds — both the aircraft and everything inside it are in free fall, and the passengers experience weightlessness, just as the stone would have done if it could feel anything.

The parabola has another surprising property, which we also met in Chapter 8: its focus. Rays that come in parallel to the parabola's axis are all reflected into the same focus — no matter where on the curve they strike.

That is why parabolic mirrors are used in solar furnaces, where the Sun's rays are collected at one point with enormous heat, in radio telescopes, which pick up faint radio signals from outer space, and in satellite dishes, which collect a TV signal in the receiver sitting exactly at the focus.

We also met the parabola in the previous chapter, in the main cable of the suspension bridge. When the roadway is hung up and spreads its weight evenly over the cable's horizontal length, the cable takes the shape of exactly a parabola. Some arch bridges use the same curve the other way round: a parabolic arch that carries the pressure from below instead of the pull from above.

The ellipse — planetary orbits and periodic comets

Kepler showed more than 400 years ago that the planets move in ellipses around the Sun, with the Sun at one focus. The same holds for most of the comets we know — just with far more elongated ellipses that send them very far away from the Sun and back again.

I have followed the comet 10P/Tempel 2 with my own telescope and calculated its orbit from my own observations. Here you see the result.

10P/Tempel 2 is what is called a periodic comet — its orbit is a closed ellipse, like the planets', but much more elongated. It comes closest to the Sun (its perihelion) inside the orbit of Mars, and farthest away (its aphelion) out beyond the orbit of Jupiter, about 4.6 AU from the Sun.

This is what a comet actually looks like through a telescope: a small, fuzzy patch, almost like a star that has run a little. But from exactly this kind of image — taken night after night — you can work out the shape of the whole orbit, as the diagram above shows.

Parabolic and hyperbolic orbits — visitors from outside

Not all comets are periodic. Some pass the Sun once only and disappear for ever. Which orbit an object follows depends solely on how fast it is moving.

DEFINITION

If the speed is low enough, the orbit becomes an ellipse — the object is captured by the Sun's gravity and returns again and again. If the object has exactly the escape velocity — the precise speed needed to get away from the Sun's gravity for ever — the orbit becomes a parabola. If the speed is higher than the escape velocity, the orbit becomes a hyperbola, and the object leaves the Solar System never to return.

REMEMBER

A hyperbolic orbit tells us something important: the object cannot have been born in our Solar System. Had it always belonged here, the Sun's gravity could never have given it more than exactly the escape velocity — that is, at most a parabolic orbit. If the speed is higher than that, the object must have come from outside, from interstellar space, and is just passing by on its way onwards.

It has actually happened: in 2017 astronomers discovered 'Oumuamua, and in 2019 the comet 2I/Borisov — both on clearly hyperbolic orbits. The two are the first confirmed visitors from another solar system that we have ever observed.

From a thrown stone to a comet millions of kilometres away — the same four curves, the circle, the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola, turn up again and again. That is one of the most beautiful things about geometry: some of the very simplest shapes there are turn out to govern almost everything around us.

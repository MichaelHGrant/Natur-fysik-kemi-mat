My degree from DTH – civilingeniør in acoustics, 1989, revisited and brought up to date

In January 1989 I was awarded my diploma as civilingeniør – Master of Science in Engineering – by the Technical University of Denmark (DTH, today DTU) in Lyngby, with a thesis at the Acoustics Laboratory. As with the courses of my B.Sc. from Sussex, I have recreated the courses – not as exam notes, but as they look today: with the physics and history behind them, interactive figures to experiment with, and what has happened in each field since 1989. Every page can also be downloaded as a PDF.

DTH in the 1980s

The Technical University of Denmark was founded in 1829 as the Polytechnic Institute (Den Polytekniske Læreanstalt), with H. C. Ørsted as its first director. In the 1960s and 70s it moved from Copenhagen to the large campus at Lyngby, and in 1994 it took the name Danmarks Tekniske Universitet (DTU).

In the 1980s there was no bachelor’s degree in Denmark, and the engineering degree was not divided into a bachelor’s and a master’s stage. It was a single, continuous course of study, normally lasting five years and requiring 330 points, which led to the title civilingeniør, polyteknisk kandidat – rendered on the diploma in English as Master of Science in Engineering. The course was built up of courses that each carried a number of points – most of them 6 – and students could put together much of their programme themselves within a field of study. A thesis completed the degree. The split into bachelor’s and master’s degrees came only later.

The Acoustics Laboratory was a strong research environment with close ties to Brüel & Kjær, Bang & Olufsen and the hearing-aid industry. In the early 1980s it helped develop the measurement of sound intensity with two microphones, and in the middle of the decade work began on the room-acoustics program ODEON. Today acoustics at DTU is part of DTU Electro.

The special study plan

I came to DTH with a B.Sc. from the University of Sussex. As there was no Danish bachelor’s stage to be admitted to, I applied for a dispensation, and on 4 December 1984 the Polytechnic Institute approved a special study plan (særstudieplan) for me. I was not, however, given credit for all my Sussex courses – partly because it was a science degree rather than an engineering degree. The special study plan required at least 198 points, of which at least 30 for the thesis, and the basic courses that the Sussex degree covered – mathematical analysis and linear algebra, mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics, atomic and nuclear physics, solid-state physics, relativity, wave physics, laser engineering and plasma physics 1 – carried no points for me. For comparison, the engineering degree normally required 330 points. The diploma states that the degree was completed under the special study plan with a reduced requirement of 168 points. Grades were given on the Danish 13-point scale. The diploma of 31 January 1989 declares me civilingeniør, polyteknisk kandidat – in English, Master of Science in Engineering.

My supervisor for the thesis, from September 1988 to January 1989, was Finn Jacobsen, one of the pioneers of sound-intensity measurement, whose lecture notes on sound propagation in pipes and ducts are among the thesis’s sources.

The courses are arranged here by subject rather than by year. Fifteen of the pages were written for this collection; three courses – plasma physics, electron spin resonance and probability – already had their own pages on the site.

  • Building-acoustics design (5147, 1988, 6 points, integrated course) – treated together with building and room acoustics.
  • Laboratory exercises in electrophysics (4077, 1986, 6 points) – see circuit theory and field theory.

  • Individual course at the Department of Process Engineering (1988, 6 points).

Sound propagation in ducts with absorbent linings

What has happened since 1989?

The biggest change is computing power. In 1989 I solved Scott’s equation frequency by frequency in HP BASIC; today a laptop computes whole sound fields in three dimensions by the finite element method, simulates concert halls so that they can be heard before they are built, and analyses measurements in real time. The measurement microphone has been joined by MEMS microphones in billions of phones, analogue electronics by signal processors, and hearing aids, loudspeakers and ultrasound scanners have become small computers. The electron microscope can now see single atoms, and chemistry has taken a leading role in the green transition. But the foundations – the wave equation, Maxwell’s equations, Fourier and Kirchhoff – are the same, and that is what these pages are about.

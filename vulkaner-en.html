Volcanoes: why some flow and others explode

Magma is molten rock with dissolved water and other gases. As the magma rises, the pressure falls and the gas forms bubbles, just as when you open a fizzy drink. In fluid basalt the bubbles escape, and the lava flows out quietly or sprays up in fountains. In viscous, silica-rich magma the bubbles are trapped until the magma shatters into ash and is shot up in a column that can reach 40 km. Basalt has about 50 % SiO₂ and erupts at 1100–1200 °C. Rhyolite has over 70 % and is only 750–850 °C. Silica links up in long chains, which is why rhyolite is a million times more viscous than basalt. The ascent speed decides whether the gas has time to seep out: if a viscous magma rises slowly, it builds a lava dome; if it rises fast, it explodes.

At the bottom the column is heavier than the air and is carried only by its speed out of the crater. It draws in cold air, which is heated and expands. If the column becomes lighter than the air before its speed is used up, it rises on as a hot bubble. Otherwise it collapses.

The world's volcanoes

Most volcanoes lie at plate boundaries. At the trenches an ocean plate slides beneath another and carries water down with it. The water lowers the melting point of the mantle above the plate, and andesite and dacite form: the Ring of Fire around the Pacific. At the mid-ocean ridges and in rift zones the plates are pulled apart, and the mantle melts as the pressure falls. Over hotspots such as Hawaii and Iceland, hot mantle rises from deep below. A large explosion shoots sulphur dioxide up into the stratosphere. There, within a few weeks, it turns into tiny droplets of sulphuric acid that scatter sunlight back into space. The droplets slowly fall out over one to two years. Pinatubo sent up about 18 million tonnes of SO₂, and the Earth's mean temperature fell by about 0.4 °C the year after. Tambora in 1815 gave the 'year without a summer' in 1816 in Europe and North America. The model is a simple energy balance with the upper layer of the ocean and the deep ocean.

Why magma rises

The mantle is solid rock, but it melts where the pressure falls or where water is added. The melt is lighter than the rock around it and is squeezed up through cracks. It gathers in a magma chamber a few kilometres beneath the volcano. When the pressure in the chamber becomes large enough, the roof cracks, and the magma rises through a conduit or a dyke.

The gas makes the difference

Water dissolves in magma just as carbon dioxide dissolves in a fizzy drink. The amount dissolved roughly follows

c = s·√P, s ≈ 4.1·10⁻⁶ Pa−½

At 100 MPa, about 4 km down, the magma can hold 4 % water. On the way up the pressure falls, and the water comes out as bubbles. At atmospheric pressure a kilogram of steam at 900 °C takes up more than 5 m³. When the bubbles fill about 75 % of the volume, the magma shatters into ash and gas that race up at several hundred metres per second.

In fluid basalt the bubbles rise faster than the magma and escape. Large bubbles bursting in the crater give Strombolian eruptions. If the magma rises fast, the gas stays in it and sprays it up in Hawaiian fountains. In viscous magma the bubbles cannot escape. If the magma rises slowly, the gas still seeps out through cracks, and the degassed magma is squeezed out as a lava dome. If it rises fast, it explodes.

log10 η = A + B/(T − C)

The viscosity η is computed here with a formula of the Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann type. B and C grow with the silica content, and dissolved water lowers the viscosity strongly. Crystals make andesite and dacite even more viscous.

The eruption column

Just above the crater the mixture of ash and gas is several times heavier than the air and is carried only by its speed. The column sucks in air from the sides. The air is heated by the ash and expands, so the column becomes lighter. If the column has become lighter than the air before its speed is used up, it rises on as an enormous hot bubble until it has the same density as the air around it. There it spreads out like an umbrella and drifts with the wind. The model here solves the equations for mass, momentum and energy in the column on the way up (Morton, Taylor & Turner 1956; Woods 1988), with an entrainment coefficient of 0.09:

d(ρub²)/dz = 2α ρa u b, d(ρu²b²)/dz = g (ρa − ρ) b²

The column height grows only as the fourth root of the mass flux: ten thousand times more magma gives a column ten times higher. Mastin et al. (2009) found H ≈ 2.0 km · (V/(m³/s))0.241 from many observed eruptions.

If the mass flux is large and the speed small, the column does not become light enough. Then it collapses like a fountain, and the ash pours down the sides of the volcano as pyroclastic flows: glowing avalanches of ash, gas and rock that race along at 100–700 km/h at 300–800 °C. Such a flow wiped out the town of Saint-Pierre on Martinique in 1902, and Pompeii and Herculaneum in AD 79.

VEI: the size of eruptions

The Volcanic Explosivity Index VEI (Newhall and Self 1982) is based on the volume of material thrown out. Each step is ten times more: VEI 4 is 0.1 km³, VEI 5 is 1 km³, VEI 6 is 10 km³ and VEI 8 more than 1000 km³. Quiet basalt eruptions have VEI 0–1, even if they pour out many km³ of lava. Laki in 1783 poured out 15 km³ of lava.

Iceland and Denmark

The Laki fissure in Iceland opened in June 1783 and for eight months poured out lava and about 120 million tonnes of sulphur dioxide. A poisonous haze settled over Europe that summer. In Iceland more than half of the livestock and about a fifth of the people died in the famine that followed. Iceland was then ruled from Denmark, and in Copenhagen there was talk of moving the Icelanders to Jutland. In April 2010 the ash from Eyjafjallajökull closed the airspace over most of Europe for a week, Denmark included, because the ash can melt inside jet engines.

Plate tectonics: the continents wander

The Earth's outer shell is divided into large plates that move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Over 400 million years that adds up to the whole way round the globe. Follow the continents from the supercontinent Rodinia 750 million years ago, through Pangaea, to today, and on 250 million years into the future, when they may come together again. Watch the Atlantic open, India hit Asia, and Denmark travel from near the South Pole to where it lies now. Drag the globe to turn it.

Denmark's journey

The place where Denmark lies today was near the South Pole 600 million years ago, in the southern subtropics in the Silurian, crossed the equator in the Carboniferous and has moved north ever since. The climate followed: tropical swamp forests, desert, a warm chalk sea and finally ice ages. In Scotese's future Denmark stays at about the same latitude, but moves east.

Continents that fit together

Even on the first good world maps you could see that the east coast of South America fits the west coast of Africa. In 1912 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener gathered the evidence that the continents had once been joined in one continent, which he called Pangaea: the same mountain ranges and rocks on both sides of the Atlantic, fossils of land animals and plants such as the reptile Mesosaurus and the seed fern Glossopteris in South America, Africa, India and Antarctica alike, and traces of ice ages in lands that now lie in the tropics. But Wegener could not explain what force should push the continents through the solid sea floor, and most geologists rejected the idea.

The sea floor spreads

The answer came from the sea floor. After the Second World War the mid-ocean ridges were mapped, a mountain chain 65,000 km long that winds through all the oceans. In 1962 Harry Hess proposed that new sea floor forms at the ridges and spreads out to both sides. In 1963 Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews showed how this can be read: when the lava solidifies, it is magnetised in the direction of the Earth's magnetic field, and the field reverses at irregular intervals. The sea floor is therefore striped with alternately normal and reversed magnetisation, in the same pattern on both sides of the ridge. See it in the section above. The stripes are a tape recording of the Earth's magnetic field, and the spacing between them gives the speed.

Plates and boundaries

At the end of the 1960s it all came together as plate tectonics. The lithosphere, the crust and the uppermost mantle down to about 100 km, is divided into a dozen large and many small rigid plates that glide over the soft asthenosphere. Almost all geological activity happens at the boundaries: at the spreading ridges the plates move apart, at the subduction zones sea floor dives into the mantle beneath another plate, and at the transform faults the plates slide past each other. When two continents collide, neither can sink, and the crust is squeezed up into high mountains such as the Himalaya.

The plates are driven mostly by their own weight. Old sea floor is cold and heavy, and where it sinks into a subduction zone, it pulls the rest of the plate with it. The fastest plates today, like the Pacific Plate, are those with long subduction zones. The ridges also push a little, because they stand high. Beneath it all the mantle flows slowly, hot rock rising and cold rock sinking, at a speed of a few centimetres a year.

Before Pangaea: Rodinia and Snowball Earth

Pangaea was not the first supercontinent. About 1000 million years ago most continents were gathered in Rodinia, which began to split about 750 million years ago. As it did, the Earth went through the coldest periods we know of: in the Cryogenian, 720–635 million years ago, the ice reached all the way to the equator, a snowball Earth. After the ice came the first large multicellular animals in the Ediacaran and then the Cambrian explosion of animal life 539 million years ago. Through the Palaeozoic the Iapetus Ocean closed, and the continents came together again until Pangaea was complete in the Carboniferous.

Supercontinents come and go

Pangaea assembled in the Carboniferous about 320 million years ago, when Gondwana in the south collided with Laurussia in the north. In the Triassic, about 200 million years ago, it began to split. Huge lava eruptions along the rift are linked to one of the great mass extinctions. First the central Atlantic opened, then the South Atlantic about 130 million years ago, and about 55 million years ago Greenland separated from Europe. India broke away from Gondwana, sailed north at up to 15 cm a year and hit Asia about 50 million years ago. This is an example of the Wilson cycle: oceans open and close, and supercontinents assemble and break up again every few hundred million years.

The future: a new supercontinent

The plates are still moving, and their motion can be projected forward. The next few tens of millions of years are fairly certain: Africa presses against Europe and closes the Mediterranean, Australia reaches South-East Asia, and the Atlantic gets wider. Further out it becomes guesswork, because new subduction zones can form and reverse the motion. Christopher Scotese has built the future the page shows: in about 150 million years the sea floor along the east coast of the Americas begins to sink, the Atlantic closes, and in 250 million years the continents are gathered again in one supercontinent, Pangaea Proxima, with a remnant of the Indian Ocean as an inland sea in the middle.

Other researchers have other ideas. If it is the Pacific that closes, while the Atlantic keeps opening, the result is Novopangaea. If both oceans close, it becomes Aurica. And if the continents gather round the North Pole, as Asia and America meet across the Arctic, it becomes Amasia. What they share is that supercontinents seem to return every few hundred million years.

Denmark on the move

420 million years ago the old continent of Baltica, to which Denmark belongs, collided with Avalonia and Laurentia. That raised the Caledonian mountains, whose remains are seen in Norway and Scotland. The boundary between Baltica and Avalonia lies deep beneath southern Denmark and northern Germany. In the Carboniferous our part of Europe lay at the equator, and in the swamp forests the coal seams formed that were later mined in England and Germany. In the Permian we lay in the desert belt in the middle of Pangaea, and when the Zechstein Sea evaporated, it left the salt domes that lie beneath Jutland today. In the Cretaceous Denmark lay under a warm sea, where chalk algae formed the chalk of Møns Klint and Stevns Klint. When the North Atlantic opened, volcanic ash rained down over Denmark and is seen today as the dark layers in the moler cliffs on Fur.

What the page shows

The positions of the continents are Christopher Scotese's PALEOMAP model, computed with the GPlates software from the EarthByte group in Sydney. It runs from 1100 million years ago to 250 million years into the future and uses a palaeomagnetic frame, so the latitudes match the climate zones. Each of the model's plates has a rotation about an axis through the Earth's centre for every time, and the page rotates the coastlines with it. The plate speeds are computed from the model's rotations. The plate boundaries for the last 410 million years come from the model of Matthews et al. (2016) and are moved into Scotese's frame by following Africa, so they fit best around Africa and less well far from it. Denmark's track is the point 56.2° N, 10.2° E, carried with its plate.

Limitations

The further back, the more uncertain the reconstruction. Sea floor older than about 200 million years has disappeared into the subduction zones, so before the Jurassic the model relies on palaeomagnetism, fossils and geology on the continents, and in the Precambrian it is very uncertain. The future is one proposal among several. Palaeomagnetism gives latitude but not longitude, so the east–west position of the continents is the most uncertain. The coastlines are today's, and mountain ranges that have worn down, or crust that has been stretched or squeezed, are not included.
